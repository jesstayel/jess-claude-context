## What Has Shifted — Session 2

Session 2 went deeper. We named the structural pattern that was keeping you stuck, and we gave it a framework you can use going forward.

### The Double Contract

Every senior engagement involves two contracts, not one. The first is the **explicit contract** — the scope of work, the deliverables, the timeline. This is what gets written down and signed. The second is the **implicit contract** — the unspoken agreement about your role, your authority, and how you will be used.

Your explicit contract was strong. Your implicit contract was working against you.

The implicit contract you had been operating under positioned you as a resource to be deployed rather than an advisor to be consulted. Sponsors were bringing you in after decisions had been made, rather than before. You were being asked to execute a strategy rather than shape it.

Changing this requires renegotiating the implicit contract — and that negotiation happens not in a formal meeting, but in the small moments: how you show up in the first conversation, what questions you ask, what you choose to comment on, what you decline to simply execute without challenge.

### The Wrong Tent Problem

We also named a second pattern: the tendency to be in the wrong tent when the important conversations happen. The people who shape strategy are not always in the formal meetings — they are in the corridor conversations, the pre-meeting briefings, the informal check-ins. If you are not in those conversations, you are not in the room where it matters.

This is not about politics. It is about understanding where influence actually lives in an organisation and making sure you are present there.

### Write Their Words

One of the most practical insights from Session 2 was about sponsor enablement. The most effective way to shift your positioning is not to advocate for yourself — it is to make it easy for your sponsors to advocate for you. That means giving them the language. Write their words for them: the framing, the narrative, the way of describing your value that they can use in conversations you are not in.

This is a skill, and it is one you are already good at. The shift is in applying it deliberately and early — not as a reactive measure when you feel undervalued, but as a proactive strategy from the start of every engagement.

### The Maturity Reframe

The reframe is this: **maturity is not the problem. The ceiling is the problem.** Every organisation, every team, every leader is sitting inside a box — a set of constraints, mental models, and pain points that are keeping them at their current level. Your job is not to judge the box or try to lift them out of it by force. Your job is to **unlock the lid** — to help them see what is keeping them stuck and to create the conditions for them to move to the next level themselves.

This is a fundamentally different posture. It moves you from *consultant who delivers* to *trusted advisor who unlocks*. And it changes everything about how you position yourself, how you enter a room, and how you have conversations.

### The Central Insight: Outcome Clarity as a Multi-Level Tool

The main territory we covered in this session was **Outcome Clarity** — not as a module to be ticked off, but as a living, multi-dimensional tool that you can apply at different scales and in different contexts.

We explored five ways to practise Outcome Clarity from where you currently sit:

**1. What is clarity?** Not the definition from the module, but your own answer — what does clarity actually mean in your body, in your practice, in the way you enter a room? This is a question worth sitting with, not answering quickly.

**2. Your job is to sell clarity.** Imagine you had a badge that said: *I sell clarity.* How would that change the way you think inside a transformation program? How would it change the first question you ask when you enter a new engagement? This reframe moves you away from *I deliver change* — which is process-oriented — towards something that speaks directly to what leaders and organisations actually need.

**3. Pattern interrupt questions.** When you ask people what they want, they will give you an answer that validates their existing point of view. Pattern interrupt questions are designed to break that loop. Examples: *If we don't do this change at all, what would we miss? Is this a problem that's causing us pain, or is there an opportunity here we haven't named yet? What is the size of the effort we're about to commit to — and is it proportionate to the problem we're solving?*

You gave a brilliant example from your own work: a colleague asked you to progress a task involving pushing EDM responsibilities to a sales team via Salesforce. Before doing anything, you asked: *what is the size of the problem we're trying to fix?* The answer was 45 hours per year. The proposed solution would have cost far more than that in effort and risk. That question — *is this proportionate?* — is a pattern interrupt.

**4. Sizing the iceberg.** Most programs are organised around outputs — systems, processes, structures, campaigns. The iceberg question goes underneath all of that: *who do we need to be in order for this to work?* You ask it as: *who do we need to be for our customers in order for sales to go up? Who do we need to be in order to save costs?*

**5. Outcome clarity for yourself.** Before you can create clarity for others, you need it for yourself. What is it that you are actually trying to achieve in this engagement?
