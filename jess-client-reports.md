## Framework 1: The Wrong Tent Problem

**What it is:** The Wrong Tent Problem describes the mistake of spending your influence capital outside your immediate team (the "tent") before you have established credibility and alignment inside it. Cheryl had been doing excellent work — designing workshops, creating a services menu, building a 12-month roadmap — but presenting it externally before her own team and direct leader had fully endorsed it. Like it's just playing vanilla at the moment."

**The distinction:** The Wrong Tent Problem is not about being too good or too fast. It is about sequencing. You must win the tent before you win the room. The tent is your boss, your peers, your immediate team. The room is the business unit leaders, the sponsors, the external stakeholders. If you go to the room before you have the tent, the tent will undermine you — not necessarily through active sabotage, but through the absence of endorsement.

**The fix:** Cheryl's move is to bring Paul (her direct boss) into the loop explicitly — not as a courtesy, but as a deliberate act of giving him back the authority she inadvertently took from him by presenting the services menu in a group setting without him. The framing: "I'm part of your team and the success of me and others is all gonna be compounded to your success. So I don't want to seem like I'm stepping on toes or doing things that is not aligned. So I'm genuinely looking for your feedback and if you're happy for me to go ahead with what I've proposed, then we go at it as a team."

## Framework 2: The Double Contract

**What it is:** The Double Contract is the principle that any internal practitioner operating in a matrix or cross-functional environment has two contracts to manage simultaneously: the contract with the sponsor (the person who hired them or gave them the mandate) and the contract with the team (the people they actually need to work with to deliver value). Most practitioners manage only the first contract and wonder why the second one is always broken.

**Verbatim quote (Jess):** "The key for the first maybe three [months] — you need to be very, very deliberate even to the point of rehearsing. What is the value that you bring? **The distinction:** The Double Contract is not just about stakeholder management. It is about the nature of the commitment. The sponsor contract is about mandate and resources. The team contract is about skin in the game — what the practitioner will give and what they need in return. Jess articulates this explicitly: "This is not me going off to do solo work for you. ## Framework 6: The Integration Imperative

**What it is:** The Integration Imperative is the principle that a practitioner who operates at a level significantly above their team's capability will, by default, isolate themselves from the team — even if they are doing excellent work. Jess articulates this as a structural risk: "If you're operating at level five and they're at level one, you have by default taking yourself out of the team. You're not integrating with the team."

The practitioner needs to bring the team along — not by slowing down their own work, but by creating visible touchpoints where the team is included, credited, and developed. The goal is to be seen as a team player who happens to be excellent, not as an individual contributor who happens to be in a team.

**The fix:** Tag-team approach — Cheryl explicitly tells her team to grab her and go into meetings together. The goal is to form a cohesive unit that is built around Cheryl, supported by the business, but also visibly supported by the team.

**What it is:** The Punch Drunk Organisation describes a leadership team that has been hit by so many changes in quick succession that they have lost the capacity to think strategically or make decisions. They are not incompetent — they are overwhelmed. The result is a pattern of deferred decisions, cancelled meetings, and a general inability to act on good work that has been presented to them.

**Verbatim quote (Jess):** "They're just, they're a bit punch drunk at the moment. No one's thinking straight. So I'm just trying to look for smart ways for them to present themselves and their value that they are."

**The context:** Cheryl's leadership team had received her 12-month roadmap and engagement strategy but had not acted on them. They had promised to share the material with the broader team but had deferred. Jess's diagnosis: this is not a rejection of the work. It is a symptom of the Punch Drunk Organisation — a team so overwhelmed by leadership transitions (the EGM retiring, the incoming leader from the gas division, Paul's own uncertain future) that they cannot process new information, even when it is excellent.

**The distinction:** The Punch Drunk Organisation is not a permanent state. It is a transitional state — and it has a window. Once the new leader consolidates their position and the dust settles, the organisation will be ready to act. The practitioner's job during the punch drunk phase is not to push harder but to plant seeds — to create the conditions for action when the organisation is ready.

## Framework 2: The 90-Day Window

**What it is:** The 90-Day Window is the critical period between the announcement of a new leader and the consolidation of their position. During this window, the new leader is still forming their view of the organisation, still deciding who to trust, still looking for quick wins. This is the practitioner's best opportunity to get in front of the new leader with a clear value proposition and a demonstrated track record.

**Verbatim quote (Jess):** "By the time all of this announcement happens and then, you know, let it settle, settle for a bit, then you will, you know, you would have had planted seeds everywhere."

**The context:** Anthony (the group manager) has not yet been formally announced as the new EGM, but is the front-runner. Jess's strategic insight: the work Cheryl is doing now with the direct reports is not just about building those relationships. It is about creating the conditions for Anthony to hear about Cheryl's work from his own team — which is a far more powerful endorsement than anything Cheryl could say directly.

**The distinction:** The 90-Day Window is not about timing a pitch. It is about positioning. The practitioner who has planted seeds with the new leader's team before the leader consolidates their position is already positioned as a trusted partner — not a vendor or a resource. The window closes when the new leader has formed their view. After that, it is much harder to shift the positioning.

**The sequencing:** Cheryl's approach — building relationships with the direct reports first, then letting those relationships speak to Anthony — is the correct sequencing. It is the opposite of going directly to the top, which would be premature and potentially threatening to Paul.

## Framework 5: The Cadence and Commitment Model

**What it is:** The Cadence and Commitment Model is the practitioner's tool for structuring an internal consulting engagement so that both parties know what they are committing to, what progress looks like, and how they will communicate. **The three elements:**

2. **The progress definition** — not just the end goal, but what progress looks like at each stage

**The de-risking principle:** The Cadence and Commitment Model de-risks the engagement in three ways: it prevents the "I forgot what we agreed" problem; it prevents the "I'm only measuring you on the end goal" problem; and it prevents the comparison problem (being compared to the previous practitioner who "just did things for people").

## 1. The Shop Front vs. The Shop

**Source:** Session 1, approx. 14:50–21:00 | Dropbox: session1_audio.m4a

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "There is a difference between what's in the shop and the shop front. It's not the same thing. Your practice is what's inside the shop. Your positioning is the shop front. Both together will help you focus where your potential should be."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Transformation leaders, change managers, and consultants who are technically excellent but cannot get clients, cannot get promoted, or keep being seen as "doers" rather than leaders. They believe their practice should speak for itself. It does not. This is the most common ceiling Jess encounters across all clients — the belief that competence is sufficient for visibility.

**Value add:**
This distinction gives the client a concrete mental model for why their efforts are not translating into the outcomes they want. It separates the problem of delivery (practice) from the problem of attraction and perception (positioning), and shows that both require active, deliberate investment. It also reframes the frustration: the problem is not that the market doesn't value what they do — it is that the market cannot see what they do.

**The framework:**
The Shop Front vs. The Shop is a retail metaphor that maps directly onto the professional positioning challenge. The shop (practice) contains all the expertise, frameworks, certifications, deliverables, and technical knowledge a professional has accumulated. The shop front (positioning) is everything that causes someone to walk in — the LinkedIn headline, the way you speak about yourself at a networking event, the language you use in a proposal, the story you tell when someone asks "what do you do?"

Jess draws the analogy from the retail world deliberately: in a physical store, the people responsible for the shop front (visual merchandising, marketing, promotions) are entirely different from the people responsible for what is inside (operations, product placement, inventory). Both roles require different mindsets. The shop front person is thinking about what gets people through the door. The inside person is thinking about optimisation and delivery. Most transformation professionals are exclusively inside-the-shop thinkers who have never developed the shop-front muscle.

The critical insight is that the shop front is not a vanity exercise — it is a prerequisite for the shop to function. You can have the most extraordinary products inside, but if no one walks in, the shop does not exist commercially. And crucially: once people do walk in, they will give you feedback that helps you evolve what is inside. The shop front is what generates the market intelligence that improves the practice. Positioning and practice are not separate — they are in a feedback loop.

**Soundbite:**
> "Your practice is what's inside the shop. Your positioning is the shop front. You can't run a shop with no front."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Separate the two questions: "What do I do and how well do I do it?" (practice) vs. "How do people perceive me and what draws them to me?" (positioning).
2. Audit your current positioning: search yourself on LinkedIn, Google, ask a trusted peer what they would say you do. Is the shop front clear, compelling, and distinct?
3. Identify the gap: what is the one thing your shop front is not communicating that your practice actually delivers?
4. Begin building the shop front as a deliberate discipline — not a one-time task, but an ongoing investment equal to the investment in practice.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The most common mistake is believing that "people who know me know my value." This is true — but it only works for repeat business. The shop front is for people who do not yet know you.
- Positioning does not mean self-promotion in the uncomfortable sense. It means translating your value into language that resonates with the person who needs it — before they know they need you.
- The shop front also protects you from being boxed in. If your positioning is "safe pair of hands," people will keep you in that box. The shop front is where you redefine the box.

## 3. Two-Way Change Adoption

**Source:** Session 1, approx. 58:00–59:30 | Dropbox: session1_audio.m4a

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "Adoption is not just adopting to the system, but change adoption is that the system has not adopted to the problems of the business. So it's two ways. They have not adopted to the system, but the bigger problem is the system has not adopted to us."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Change managers and transformation leads who frame adoption as a one-directional problem — getting people to use the new system or process — and miss the more fundamental issue: that the system itself was not designed around the actual problems of the business.

**Value add:**
This reframe doubles the scope of the change adoption conversation and immediately elevates the professional from "change manager" to "strategic advisor." It also gives the professional a way to surface the real problem in a discovery conversation without sounding critical of the technical team.

**The framework:**
Change adoption has two directions:
- Direction 1 (the standard view): people adopting to the new system/process/tool.
- Direction 2 (the deeper view): the system/process/tool adopting to the real problems of the business.

Most change management work focuses exclusively on Direction 1. But Direction 2 is where the real value lies — because if the system was not designed around the actual pain (e.g., "our sales turnaround time is two weeks when it should be one"), then no amount of adoption work will create the business outcome the organisation is hoping for.

The practical implication: in any discovery conversation, the change leader needs to surface both directions. "Are people using the system?" is a Direction 1 question. "Is the system solving the problem it was supposed to solve?" is a Direction 2 question. The second question is almost always the more important one.

**Soundbite:**
> "Change adoption is not just people adopting to the system. It's also the system adopting to the people. And the bigger problem is usually the second one."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. In discovery, ask both directions: "Are people using the new system/process?" AND "Is the system actually solving the problem it was designed to solve?"
2. Surface the real pain: not "requirements were not gathered properly" but "our turnaround time is two weeks when it should be one."
3. Use Direction 2 as the entry point for the strategic conversation — it immediately elevates the discussion from implementation to business outcomes.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- Avoid using the term "change adoption" with clients — translate it into their specific pain language.
- Direction 2 is often politically sensitive because it implies the technical implementation was flawed. Frame it as "the system evolving to meet the business" rather than "the system was wrong."
- The two-way framing is also a powerful way to reframe resistance: people are not resisting change — they are resisting a system that has not yet adopted to their reality.

**Domain:**
Practice — specifically, the diagnostic and discovery phase of any transformation engagement. Also touches Positioning (the professional who asks Direction 2 questions is immediately perceived differently from the one who only asks Direction 1 questions).

## 4. "On Me vs. In Me" — The Embodiment Distinction

**Source:** Session 1, approx. 64:35–65:00 | Dropbox: session1_audio.m4a

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "Whilst this is on me, it's not in me. And I need to move it from on me to in me, where it becomes me. It's not just — I'm not being. It's a different, elevated state."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Professionals who intellectually understand a concept — they can explain it, they can teach it — but have not yet embodied it in their practice. The knowledge sits on top of them rather than being integrated into how they think, speak, and act. This is the gap between knowing and being.

**Value add:**
This distinction gives the client a precise language for the gap between intellectual understanding and genuine capability. It also reframes the work of coaching: coaching is not about adding more knowledge (putting more things "on" you) but about moving what is already there from the surface into the being state.

**The framework:**
Three states of knowing:
1. **On me** — I have learned it, I can explain it, I understand it conceptually. But it is not yet integrated into how I show up.
2. **In me** — I have practised it enough that it is part of my instinctive response. I do not have to think about it; it happens.
3. **Being it** — it is so integrated that it is indistinguishable from who I am. It is my natural state.

The coaching work is the deliberate, intentional movement from state 1 to state 2 to state 3. Most professional development stops at state 1 — the knowledge is acquired but not embodied. The danger is that state 1 feels like progress (you can talk about it, you can teach it) but does not change how you actually show up in the room.

Jess uses the football commentary analogy: you can watch a game and know exactly what the player should have done. But when you are on the field, you cannot do half of what they do — because it is not in you.

**Soundbite:**
> "It's on me. But it's not in me. And until it's in me, it's not changing anything."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. After any learning experience, ask: "Is this on me or in me?" If you can explain it but have not yet changed your behaviour, it is on you.
2. To move from on to in: practise deliberately in real situations. Do not wait for the perfect moment — use the current engagement as the practice ground.
3. Slow down. The movement from on to in requires presence and intentionality. It cannot be rushed.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The danger of staying at "on me" is that it creates a false sense of progress. You feel like you are growing because you are learning — but the behaviour has not changed.
- The football analogy is powerful for clients who are intellectually oriented (which is most transformation leaders). It makes the gap visceral and real.
- The coaching relationship is specifically designed to accelerate the on-to-in movement by creating accountability and reflection.

**Domain:**
Potential — specifically, the work of embodying what you know so that it changes how you show up. Also touches Practice (the embodied practice is what makes the difference in the room).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Skyscraper Syndrome" from Session 7 — the skyscraper is built on knowledge that is on you, not in you.

## 5. The Richness Principle — Multiple Lenses vs. One Checklist

**Source:** Session 1, approx. 59:50–60:25 | Dropbox: session1_audio.m4a

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "You have that richness and insightfulness and resourcefulness that you can pull the right things in the right sequence at the right depth with the right intensity to unlock them to what is next."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Experienced professionals who apply the same framework or checklist to every engagement, regardless of context. This produces technically correct but contextually flat work — work that does not land because it does not meet the client where they are.

**Value add:**
This principle reframes expertise from "I know the framework" to "I know which part of which framework to use, at what depth, in what sequence, with what intensity, for this specific situation." This is the difference between a practitioner and a master.

**The framework:**
The Richness Principle states that true expertise is not the accumulation of more frameworks — it is the ability to draw on your full range of knowledge and experience to select the right tool, at the right depth, in the right sequence, with the right intensity, for the specific lock in front of you.

This is contrasted with the "checklist" approach: applying a standard methodology in the same sequence regardless of context. The checklist approach is safe and defensible but rarely transformative. It produces work that is technically correct but does not create the connection or the unlock that the client actually needs.

The practical implication: before entering any engagement, the question is not "which framework do I use?" but "what is the lock, and which of my tools is the right key for this specific lock?"

**Soundbite:**
> "The right thing, in the right sequence, at the right depth, with the right intensity. That's not a checklist. That's mastery."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Before each engagement, resist the urge to reach for your default framework. Ask: "What is the specific lock here?"
2. Map your full range of tools and ask: "Which of these is the right key for this lock?"
3. Be willing to go deeper or shallower than your default — the depth and intensity should be calibrated to the client's readiness, not your comfort level.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The checklist approach is not wrong — it is a starting point. The richness principle is what you develop on top of it.
- Clients can feel the difference between a checklist and a tailored approach, even if they cannot articulate it. The tailored approach creates trust; the checklist creates compliance.
- This principle is especially important when building your own practice — because in a job, you have colleagues and a boss to course-correct you. In your own practice, you are the only one making these judgment calls.

## 1. "I Sell Clarity" — The Reframe of the Transformation Leader's Role

**Source:** Session 2, approx. 10:19–10:22 and 35:09–35:34 | Dropbox: session2_audio.m4a

**Verbatim transcript quote:**

**Pain point this addresses:**
Transformation leaders who define themselves by their methodology (change management, agile, project management) rather than by the outcome they create. This self-definition limits their positioning, their pricing, and their ability to influence — because "change manager" is a commodity, while "clarity seller" is a strategic partner.

**Value add:**
This reframe immediately elevates the professional from implementer to advisor. It also gives them a concrete, memorable identity that can be used in positioning conversations, LinkedIn content, and client proposals. It shifts the conversation from "what do you do?" to "what do you create?" — and the answer (clarity) is something every organisation desperately needs but rarely has.

**The framework:**
The "I Sell Clarity" reframe has three dimensions:
1. **Clarity for the client** — helping them see what they are actually trying to change, and why, before any implementation begins.
2. **Clarity for the stakeholders** — creating the bridge between what the change leader can see (the full iceberg) and what the stakeholders can see (only their corner of it).
3. **Clarity for the organisation** — sizing the status quo, understanding the undercurrent, and moving from outputs (what we will do) to outcomes (what we want to become).

The reframe is not just semantic. It changes the entire approach to an engagement. A change manager asks "what is the plan?" A clarity seller asks "what is really going on here?" A change manager delivers a change management plan. A clarity seller creates the conditions under which people choose to move.

**Soundbite:**
> "My job is not change. My job is to sell clarity. And clarity is the rarest thing in any transformation."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. In your next introduction or proposal, replace "change manager" or "transformation lead" with language that describes the outcome you create: "I help organisations see what they are actually trying to change — and why — before they spend a dollar on implementation."
2. In discovery conversations, orient every question toward clarity: "What does success look like?" "What would we miss if we did nothing?" "Who do we need to be for this to work?"
3. Use the clarity reframe to elevate your pricing: clarity is strategic, not tactical. Strategic advisors charge differently from implementers.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The clarity reframe works best when you can demonstrate it in the room — by asking a question that stops the conversation and makes people think. That is the shop front for this identity.
- Avoid using the word "clarity" with clients as a label — it can sound abstract. Translate it into their language: "I help you understand what you're actually trying to achieve before you start."
- The reframe also protects you from scope creep: if your role is to sell clarity, you are not the one who implements everything that clarity reveals.

**Domain:**
Positioning — specifically, the identity reframe that changes how you introduce yourself and how clients perceive your value. Also touches Practice (the clarity-selling approach changes how you conduct discovery and stakeholder engagement).

## 2. Sizing the Iceberg — Outcome Clarity as a Multi-Level Practice

**Source:** Session 2, approx. 15:03–15:12 and 24:33–26:10 | Dropbox: session2_audio.m4a

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "One of the things that are almost always missing in transformation programs and change programs is that we don't spend enough time to size the iceberg. What is it that we're actually changing? It's probably one of the easiest questions to ask — the hardest question to answer."
> "Once you get that understanding, it's almost like having a new map open up to you."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Transformation leaders who execute on what they have been told is the problem — the visible tip of the iceberg — without ever sizing the full iceberg. This produces technically correct but strategically insufficient work. The programme delivers its outputs but does not create the outcomes the organisation actually needs.

**Value add:**
Sizing the iceberg before acting gives the transformation leader a fundamentally different starting point. It changes what questions they ask, what they recommend, and how they position themselves. It also protects them from being set up to fail — because the work they are asked to do may be addressing the wrong problem entirely.

**The framework:**
The Iceberg Framework has three levels:
1. **The visible tip** — what the organisation says it wants to change (the system, the process, the structure). This is what most change work addresses.
2. **The waterline** — the undercurrent of assumptions, incentives, and behaviours that are keeping the status quo in place. This is where most change work fails.
3. **The deep iceberg** — the emotional connections, the mental models, the culture, the power dynamics, and the identity of the organisation. This is where real transformation happens.

Sizing the iceberg means understanding all three levels before recommending anything. The question "what is it that we're actually changing, and changing to what?" is the entry point to this sizing work. It is easy to ask and hard to answer — because the honest answer requires the organisation to confront what is below the waterline.

Jess also introduces the video game map analogy: at the first level, you can only see a small section of the map. As you progress, the full map opens up. Sizing the iceberg is what opens the full map — and once you can see it, you know what weapons you need, who you need to be allies with, and where the real threats are.

**Soundbite:**
> "We don't spend enough time sizing the iceberg. What is it we're actually changing? It's the easiest question to ask and the hardest to answer."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. At the start of every engagement, before any planning or design work, spend time sizing the iceberg: "What is the visible problem? What is the waterline assumption? What is the deep iceberg?"
2. Use the question "what is it that we're actually changing, and changing to what?" as the formal entry point to this sizing work.
3. Follow up with: "Who do we need to be collectively for this to work?" — this surfaces the culture and identity dimension without using the word "culture."

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The iceberg metaphor is powerful with clients because it is visual and intuitive. Most people immediately understand that what is visible is not the whole story.
- The sizing work is not a one-time activity — it needs to be revisited as the engagement progresses, because the iceberg shifts as the organisation learns.
- Outcome clarity can be applied at multiple scales: for yourself (clarity about your own role), for the problem you are in, for the stakeholders you need to move, for the entire programme. The same framework, different scales.

**Domain:**
Practice — specifically, the discovery and diagnostic phase of any transformation engagement. Also touches Positioning (the professional who sizes the iceberg is immediately perceived as more strategic than the one who just executes the plan).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Lock" concept from Session 1 — the iceberg is where the lock lives.
- Connects to the "Drone to Living Room" technique later in this session — the drone is how you see the full iceberg; the living room is where the stakeholder is sitting.
- Connects to the "Two-Way Change Adoption" from Session 1 — sizing the iceberg reveals both directions of the adoption problem.

**Diagram potential:**
Yes. A classic iceberg visual: above the waterline = "what the organisation says it wants to change"; at the waterline = "assumptions, incentives, behaviours"; below the waterline = "mental models, culture, power dynamics, identity." An arrow pointing to the full iceberg labelled "What you need to size before you start."

## 3. Pattern Interrupt Questions — Breaking the Default Answer

**Source:** Session 2, approx. 11:05–11:42 | Dropbox: session2_audio.m4a

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "Things that I call pattern interrupts. Things they wouldn't expect you to ask. Things like, if we don't do this change at all, what would we miss?"

**Pain point this addresses:**
Transformation leaders who ask the standard discovery questions and get the standard answers — which confirm the organisation's existing point of view and produce no new insight. The client says "faster horses" and the change leader designs a faster horse programme. The real opportunity is never surfaced.

**Value add:**
Pattern interrupt questions break the default answer loop. They force the client to think from a different angle — and in doing so, they often reveal the real problem, the real opportunity, or the real emotional connection that will drive change. They also immediately signal that this professional is not a standard practitioner — they think differently.

**The framework:**
Pattern interrupt questions have three characteristics:
1. **Unexpected** — the client would not have anticipated this question from a change manager.
2. **Reframing** — they shift the perspective from problem to opportunity, from action to outcome, from doing to being.
3. **Generative** — they open up new possibilities rather than confirming existing assumptions.

Examples from the session:
- "If we don't do this change at all, what would we miss?" (reframes from problem to value)
- "Is this a problem that's causing us pain, or is there an opportunity to leverage here?" (reframes from problem to opportunity)
- "Who do we need to be for this to work?" (reframes from doing to being)
- "What would make you change your mind about this?" (surfaces the emotional motive)

The key discipline is to resist the urge to ask the question that confirms the existing plan. The pattern interrupt question is the one that makes the room go quiet — because no one has asked it before.

**Soundbite:**
> "Ask the question they wouldn't expect. The silence that follows is where the real answer lives."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Before any discovery conversation, prepare three pattern interrupt questions specific to the engagement.
2. Use the "if we don't do this at all, what would we miss?" question as a standard opening — it immediately surfaces the real value of the change.
3. When a conversation is going in circles, use "is this a problem or an opportunity?" to break the loop.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- Pattern interrupt questions work best when delivered calmly and with genuine curiosity — not as a challenge or a provocation.
- The silence after a pattern interrupt question is valuable. Do not fill it. Let the client sit with it.
- Not every pattern interrupt question will land. Have three ready and use the one that fits the moment.

**Domain:**
Practice — specifically, the discovery and stakeholder engagement phase. Also touches Positioning (the professional who asks pattern interrupt questions is perceived as a strategic thinker, not a process executor).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Iceberg" framework — pattern interrupt questions are how you get below the waterline.
- Connects to the "Clarity Seller" identity — clarity sellers ask pattern interrupt questions; change managers ask standard discovery questions.

**Diagram potential:**
Yes. A two-column table: "Standard Question" vs. "Pattern Interrupt Version." Examples: "What is the problem?" vs. "If we don't do this at all, what would we miss?" / "What do you want to achieve?" vs. "Who do we need to be for this to work?"

**LinkedIn post idea:**
Hook: "The question that changed every discovery conversation I've ever had." Story: the pattern interrupt concept — the question they wouldn't expect, the silence that follows, and what it reveals. CTA: "What's your pattern interrupt question?"

**Reel / YouTube video idea:**

## 4. The Drone to Living Room — The Influence Technique

**Source:** Session 2, approx. 45:15–45:33 | Dropbox: session2_audio.m4a

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "It's good to be into that balance between big picture and dropping from the drone to the living room. Drone, living room. If you want influence, that's what influence looks like."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Transformation leaders who can see the full picture (the drone view) but cannot get stakeholders to act because the stakeholders are in their living room — they can only see what is directly in front of them. The change leader reports the problem from the drone but the stakeholder cannot see it from where they are sitting.

**Value add:**
This technique gives the transformation leader a concrete method for creating influence: move between the drone view (the full iceberg, the strategic picture) and the living room (the stakeholder's immediate reality, their KPIs, their emotional connection). The bridge between these two views is where influence is created.

**The framework:**
The Drone to Living Room technique has two moves:
1. **The drone** — the change leader's view of the full situation: the iceberg, the risks, the opportunities, the systemic patterns. This is what they can see that the stakeholder cannot.
2. **The living room** — the stakeholder's view: their immediate priorities, their KPIs, their team, their emotional connection to the status quo.

Influence is created by moving deliberately between these two views:
- Start in the living room: acknowledge what the stakeholder can see and cares about.
- Lift to the drone: show them what you can see from above — but only the part that is relevant to what they care about.
- Land back in the living room: connect the drone view to their immediate reality and give them a reason to act.

The critical mistake is staying at the drone level — reporting the full picture without connecting it to the living room. This produces a "so what?" response. The stakeholder acknowledges the problem but does not act because it has not been connected to what they care about.

**Soundbite:**
> "You've got the drone view. They're in the living room. Influence is the art of moving between the two."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Before any stakeholder conversation, identify: "What is their living room right now? What are they focused on? What do they care about?"
2. Prepare the bridge: "What from my drone view is most relevant to their living room?"
3. In the conversation, start in the living room, lift briefly to the drone, and land back in the living room with a specific ask or recommendation.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The drone view without the living room landing is just reporting. It does not create influence.
- The living room without the drone view is just empathy. It does not create movement.
- The bridge between the two is where the change leader's unique value lives — and it requires both the strategic view and the relational intelligence to connect them.

**Domain:**
Practice — specifically, stakeholder influence and engagement. Also touches Positioning (the professional who can move between drone and living room is perceived as both strategic and relational — a rare combination).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Iceberg" framework — the drone is how you see the full iceberg; the living room is where the stakeholder is sitting on it.
- Connects to the "Bridge" concept later in this session — the drone-to-living-room movement is how you build the bridge.

**Diagram potential:**
Yes. A vertical visual: at the top, a drone with a wide-angle view of the full landscape (labelled "Your view — the full iceberg, the risks, the systemic patterns"). At the bottom, a living room with a person looking out a window at a small section of the landscape (labelled "Their view — their KPIs, their team, their immediate reality"). An arrow moving between the two labelled "Influence."

**LinkedIn post idea:**
Hook: "I had the full picture. They couldn't see it. Here's why." Story: the drone-to-living-room technique — the gap between what the change leader sees and what the stakeholder sees, and how to bridge it. CTA: "Are you reporting from the drone or influencing from the living room?"

**Reel / YouTube video idea:**

**The framework:**
The GP vs. Specialist Chiropractor metaphor maps the transformation landscape:

## 1. The Menu Trap — Why Everything You Know Cannot Be Everything You Offer

**Source:** Session 3, approx. 16:09–17:56 | Dropbox: session3_video.mp4

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "What you know and your offerings are two very different things and you cannot — the first way to lose the battle, lose the dream is when you start to become a menu."
> "Cannot be a menu. Not everything you know is everything you want to offer. First, because it dilutes your value. Second is because it's no longer an organisation you're working within and you're coming in as the experts, they're expecting a lot of depth and with a very short runway."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Professionals leaving corporate who translate their entire career history into a list of services — "I can help with change management, transformation, strategy, P&L, operating model, customer experience..." This is the most common mistake Jess sees in the transition from corporate to practice, and it is the fastest way to become a commodity.

**Value add:**
This distinction saves professionals from the most expensive mistake in building a practice: trying to be everything to everyone. It also reframes the challenge: the question is not "what can I offer?" but "what is the one thing that people would specifically seek me out for?" That is the niche. Everything else is noise.

**The framework:**
The Menu Trap has three consequences:
1. **Value dilution** — when you offer everything, you are implicitly saying you are not the best at anything. The specialist commands a premium; the generalist competes on price.
2. **Credibility risk** — as an external consultant, the runway is short and the stakes are high. Clients are paying for depth. A menu signals breadth, not depth.
3. **Identity confusion** — the menu makes it impossible for people to refer you, because they cannot explain what you do in one sentence. Referrals require simplicity.

The antidote is the niche — a specific problem, for a specific type of client, that you solve in a specific way that no one else does. The niche is not a limitation; it is a magnet. It is what makes people say "I want this solution to solve that problem from that person."

**Soundbite:**
> "The first way to lose the battle is to become a menu. Not everything you know is everything you want to offer."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. List everything you know and everything you have done in the last 18 months.
2. Ask: "Which of these do I thrive on the most? Which creates the most depth of insight? Which do I do differently from everyone else?"
3. From that list, identify the one thing that people would specifically seek you out for — not the thing you can do, but the thing you do that no one else does at the same depth.
4. Build your offering around that one thing. Everything else is either a door-opener or a future expansion.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The menu is seductive because it feels safe — if you offer everything, surely someone will want something. But it produces the opposite effect: when people cannot categorise you, they do not hire you.
- The niche does not mean you only do one thing forever. It means you lead with one thing, build credibility in that thing, and then expand from a position of strength.
- The transition from menu to niche is uncomfortable because it requires saying no to work you could do. That discomfort is the signal that you are doing it right.

**Domain:**
Positioning — specifically, the design of the practice offering. Also touches Practice (the niche is where your deepest capability lives) and Potential (the niche is what creates the ceiling-breaking trajectory).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Shop Front vs. Shop" from Session 1 — the niche is the shop front; the menu is a shop with no front.
- Connects to the "GP vs. Specialist Chiropractor" from Session 2 — the specialist is the niche; the GP is the menu.
- Connects to the "Compelling Profile" work in Session 4 — the compelling profile is built on the niche, not the menu.

**Diagram potential:**

## 2. The Internal vs. External Perception Gap — Why Leaving Changes Everything

**Source:** Session 3, approx. 35:27–36:47 | Dropbox: session3_video.mp4

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "Why do you think people bring in externals first? Because of credibility. Second is because they have an eye that is different from the internal — that's assumed. Once you are internal, you become one of us. They rock the boat, you can't really say what you're really thinking. But the expectation completely shifts if you're external."
> "In internal, by default, your role is a cog in the system. You are a resource on the line in a plan."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Professionals who have been saying the same things internally for months or years — and being ignored — and then discover that the moment they become external, the same insights are treated as gold. This is not a coincidence. It is a structural feature of how organisations perceive internal vs. external voices.

**Value add:**
Understanding this structural gap gives the professional two things: (1) the explanation for why their internal influence was limited — it was not about the quality of their insight, it was about their structural position; and (2) the motivation to make the external move — because the same insight, delivered from an external position, will land differently.

**The framework:**
The Internal vs. External Perception Gap has three dimensions:
1. **Credibility** — externals are assumed to be experts; internals are assumed to be advocates for their team or function.
2. **Permission** — externals are expected to be direct, challenge assumptions, and deliver tough love. Internals are expected to navigate politics and protect relationships.
3. **Identity** — externals are hired to solve a specific problem; internals are resources on a plan. The external's identity is their expertise; the internal's identity is their role.

The practical implication: the same insight, delivered by the same person, will land differently depending on whether they are internal or external. This is not fair — but it is real. And it is one of the most powerful reasons to build a practice.

**Soundbite:**
> "You've been saying the same things for months. They ignored you. You left. Now they call you for guidance. That's not irony — that's the internal-external perception gap."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Before making the move to practice, identify the insights and perspectives that have been consistently ignored internally. These are often the most valuable ones — and they will land differently from an external position.
2. In your practice positioning, explicitly reference the external perspective as a feature: "I bring an outside view that internal teams cannot provide."
3. When repositioning within a current engagement (e.g., from a project that has been put on hold to the broader programme), use the external lens deliberately: "I can see things from outside the programme structure that the internal team cannot."

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The internal-external gap is not just about credibility — it is about permission. Externals are given permission to say things that internals cannot. Use that permission deliberately.
- The gap also applies to pricing: externals can charge for the same work that internals do for free. The value is not different — the structural position is.
- The danger of going back internal after building a practice: you lose the permission and the credibility that the external position gives you. This is why the transition needs to be deliberate and well-timed.

**Domain:**
Positioning — specifically, the structural advantage of the external position. Also touches Potential (the external position is what enables the ceiling-breaking trajectory).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "It doesn't matter how good the transformation if you don't have Jess" positioning later in this session.
- Connects to the "Unspoken Role" concept from Session 7 (TLI onboarding) — the external position is where the unspoken role becomes visible.

**Diagram potential:**
Yes. A two-column table: "Internal" vs. "External" with three rows: Credibility (assumed advocate vs. assumed expert), Permission (navigate politics vs. deliver tough love), Identity (resource on a plan vs. expert solving a specific problem).

**LinkedIn post idea:**
Hook: "I said the same thing for 18 months. They ignored me. I left. They called me the next week for advice." Story: the internal-external perception gap — why the same insight lands differently depending on your structural position. CTA: "What are you saying internally that would land differently from the outside?"

**Reel / YouTube video idea:**

## 3. "It Doesn't Matter How Good the Transformation If You Don't Have Jess" — The Non-Competitive Positioning

**Source:** Session 3, approx. 53:28–54:13 | Dropbox: session3_video.mp4

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "He said something that I have never forgot. He said, it doesn't matter how good the transformation, if you don't have Jess on the transformation, it's money down the drain."
> "So I can work with all the other consultants, advisors and stuff. It's not pick me or them. It's pick whoever you want. And I'm coming a long way anyway. That's a very different positioning than choose me or him or her."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Professionals who position themselves in competition with other consultants — which forces a comparison on price, credentials, and experience. This is a losing game for anyone who is not the biggest, most credentialed, or cheapest option.

**Value add:**
The non-competitive positioning reframes the entire conversation. Instead of "choose me over them," the message becomes "whatever you bring, I complete." This is the positioning of a category of one — someone who is not competing in the existing market but has created their own.

**The framework:**
The Non-Competitive Positioning has three elements:
1. **Complementarity** — you are not a replacement for other consultants; you are the thing that makes other consultants' work land. "It doesn't matter how good the transformation if you don't have Jess."
2. **Inevitability** — you are not asking to be chosen; you are positioning yourself as the inevitable addition to any serious transformation. "I'm coming a long way anyway."
3. **Category of one** — you are not competing in the existing market; you have created a category that only you occupy. "I am in my own league."

This positioning is the most powerful form of differentiation because it makes the comparison irrelevant. The question is no longer "why should I choose you over the other consultant?" It is "how do I make sure I have you on this transformation?"

**Soundbite:**
> "It doesn't matter how good the transformation. If you don't have the right person on the human side, it's money down the drain. That's not competitive positioning. That's category of one."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Identify the thing that you do that makes other people's work land — the human element, the adoption element, the clarity element that is missing from most transformations.
2. Build your positioning around that thing: "I don't compete with your consultants. I complete them."
3. In proposals and conversations, explicitly reference the complementarity: "Whatever you bring in, I work alongside. I am the thing that makes the rest of it work."

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- This positioning requires confidence — because you are explicitly saying that without you, the transformation is at risk. That is a bold claim. It needs to be backed up by evidence and stories.
- The non-competitive positioning also protects you from price comparisons: if you are not competing with the other consultant, you cannot be compared on price.
- The "I'm coming a long way anyway" framing is powerful because it signals that you are not desperate for the work — you are choosing to be there. That changes the power dynamic entirely.

**Domain:**
Positioning — specifically, the design of a non-competitive positioning that creates a category of one. Also touches Practice (the complementary role is a specific practice design choice) and Potential (the category-of-one positioning is what enables premium pricing and global reach).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Shop Front vs. Shop" from Session 1 — the non-competitive positioning is the most powerful shop front.
- Connects to the "Clarity Seller" identity from Session 2 — the clarity seller is the complement to every other consultant.
- Connects to the "Compelling Profile" work in Session 4 — the compelling profile is built on the non-competitive positioning.

Outside the overlap, a separate circle labelled "You — the thing that makes the overlap work." An arrow from the outer circle to the overlap labelled "Complementarity."

## 4. The 18-Month Reflection Exercise — The Foundation Document

**Source:** Session 3, approx. 31:44–38:10 | Dropbox: session3_video.mp4

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "I want you to create this document that has three questions in it. What do I call myself on those in the 18 months? What did people think you are actually helping them with? What do you think you are helping the business with? Where do you see the gap? If you were to redo the whole thing again, would you do anything differently?"
> "That intersection — where you think you're being perceived and how they actually perceive you — is where we find the 'do not do' in the practice."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Professionals who are building a practice without a clear understanding of the gap between how they see themselves and how others see them. This gap is the source of most positioning failures — because the practice is built on self-perception rather than market perception.

**Value add:**
The 18-month reflection exercise is a structured way to surface the gap between self-perception and market perception — and to use that gap to define the "do not do" that becomes the grounding rule for the practice. It is also a way to extract the positioning language from the client's own experience, rather than imposing a generic framework.

**The framework:**
The 18-Month Reflection Exercise has five questions:
1. **What did I call myself?** — the title, the role, the identity I presented.
2. **What did people think I was helping them with?** — the market perception of my value.
3. **What do I think I was helping the business with?** — my self-perception of my value.
4. **Where is the gap?** — the intersection between questions 2 and 3. This is where the "do not do" lives.
5. **If I were to redo it, what would I do differently?** — the forward-looking insight that shapes the practice design.

The "do not do" is the most important output of this exercise. It is the set of things that the professional should explicitly exclude from their practice — not because they cannot do them, but because doing them dilutes the niche, confuses the market, and keeps the professional in the commodity trap.

**Soundbite:**
> "The gap between how you think you're perceived and how they actually perceive you — that's where you find the 'do not do.' And the 'do not do' is the grounding rule for everything you offer."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Create the document. Answer all five questions honestly, drawing on specific projects and specific feedback.
2. Pay particular attention to question 2 — what did people think you were helping them with? This is often different from what you thought you were doing.
3. From the gap (question 4), extract the "do not do" list: the things that people expected from you that you should not be offering in your practice.
4. Keep the document alive — update it after every engagement. It becomes the living record of your practice positioning.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The "do not do" is counterintuitive — most professionals want to add to their offering, not subtract. But the subtraction is what creates the niche.
- The exercise is most powerful when done with external input — asking clients and colleagues what they thought you were doing, not just reflecting on your own perception.
- The recording from Veronique's last day (where colleagues described what they loved about working with her) is a perfect input to this exercise. The language they use is the language of the market.

**Domain:**
Positioning — specifically, the diagnostic work that precedes practice design. Also touches Practice (the "do not do" shapes the practice offering) and Potential (the reflection exercise is what enables the leap from corporate to practice).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Menu Trap" earlier in this session — the "do not do" is the antidote to the menu.
- Connects to the "Compelling Profile" work in Session 4 — the compelling profile is built on the output of this exercise.

**Diagram potential:**
Yes. A Venn diagram: two circles — "How I think I'm perceived" and "How they actually perceive me." The overlap is labelled "The gap — where the 'do not do' lives." Outside the overlap on each side are the things that are unique to each perception.

**LinkedIn post idea:**
Hook: "I spent 18 months doing the wrong thing. Not because I was bad at it — because it wasn't what they needed from me." Story: the 18-month reflection exercise — the gap between self-perception and market perception, and what it reveals about your practice. CTA: "What do people think you're helping them with? Is that what you think you're doing?"

## 5. Build While You Have Income — The Transition Principle

**Source:** Session 3, approx. 27:42–30:47 | Dropbox: session3_video.mp4

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "When you don't do it and you're saying, I'm okay now. I have a job. It's fine. I'll look at it next year. And then next year comes and things happen and you said, I wish I did this on the side."
> "Now is the time to do it because I've got the income. I'm not doing it out of desperation. I can take the time to do it at the right pace, doing it the right way, because I'm not relying on the income straight away."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Professionals who delay building their practice until they have left corporate — and then find themselves building under financial pressure, which produces the wrong decisions (the menu, the desperate LinkedIn posting, the price discounting). The pressure of needing income immediately forces shortcuts that undermine the practice before it starts.

**Value add:**
Building while you have income is the single most important structural advantage in practice building. It allows you to make the right decisions — the niche, the positioning, the pricing — without the distortion of financial desperation. It also means that when you do make the leap, you already have something to land on.

**The framework:**
The Transition Principle has three phases:
1. **Build while employed** — develop the foundations (positioning, niche, value proposition, business model) while still having a salary. This is the investment phase.
2. **Transition gradually** — move from employment to practice incrementally, not in a single leap. Start with side engagements, advisory work, or part-time consulting. This is the validation phase.
3. **Leap from strength** — when the practice has enough momentum to sustain itself, make the full transition. This is the growth phase.

The danger of the single leap (leaving corporate and immediately trying to build a practice from scratch) is that it forces you into the wrong mindset — "I need income now" — which produces the menu, the desperate posting, and the price discounting. The gradual transition allows you to build from a position of strength.

**Soundbite:**
> "Build it while you have income. Don't build it out of desperation. Desperation makes all the wrong decisions for you."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Start the foundation work now — positioning, niche, value proposition — while still employed. This is not a distraction from your job; it is the investment in your future.
2. Identify the smallest possible transition: what is the one thing you could offer on the side, right now, that would validate your niche?
3. Set a timeline for the gradual transition: "In 12 months, I want to have X clients and Y revenue from my practice. That is when I will make the full transition."

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The gradual transition is not a lack of commitment — it is a strategic choice. The professionals who make the leap too early are the ones who end up going back to corporate.
- The foundation work (positioning, niche, value proposition) takes time. Starting it while employed means it is ready when you need it.
- The "build while you have income" principle also applies to content and network building: start posting on LinkedIn, attending events, and building relationships now — not after you have left.

**Domain:**
Potential — specifically, the strategic management of the corporate-to-practice transition. Also touches Positioning (the transition principle is what allows the positioning to be built correctly, without the distortion of financial pressure).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Menu Trap" earlier in this session — financial desperation is what drives the menu.
- Connects to the "Skyscraper Syndrome" from Session 7 — the gradual transition is what builds the skyscraper on solid foundations.

**Diagram potential:**
Yes. A timeline visual: Phase 1 (Build while employed — positioning, niche, value proposition), Phase 2 (Gradual transition — side engagements, validation), Phase 3 (Leap from strength — full practice). An arrow showing the direction of movement, with the label "Build from strength, not desperation."

**LinkedIn post idea:**
Hook: "The worst time to build a practice is when you need the income. Here's when to start." Story: the transition principle — why building while employed is the strategic advantage that most people miss. CTA: "Are you building your practice now, or waiting until you need to?"

**Reel / YouTube video idea:**
Opening line: "Don't wait until you need the income to start building your practice. By then, it's too late to do it right." 60-second arc: the desperation trap → the gradual transition → the three phases → close with "Build it while you have income. That's when you make the right decisions."

## 1. Practice vs. Business — The Foundational Distinction

**Source:** Session 4, approx. 04:00–08:00 | Vimeo: 2025-12-11

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "A practice is based on the practice owner's intellectual property and they are the face of the business. A business is when you have people doing the work for you and you're supervising them."
> "When you want coaching with me, when you want coaching from TLI, I am the coach. There's no other coach. It's the main distinction between business and practice — it's based on my IP and I am the face."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Professionals who conflate "starting a business" with "building a practice" — and as a result, either try to scale too early (hiring people to do what they do, before the IP is established) or stay in contractor mode indefinitely (doing nine-to-five daily rate work under their own ABN, which is not a practice).

**Value add:**
The practice vs. business distinction is the first clarifying framework that allows a professional to understand what they are actually building — and to design it correctly from the start. It also clarifies the scaling path: a practice can become a business (through certification, franchising, licensing), but only after the IP is established and the practice owner has become the face of the brand.

**The framework:**
| Dimension | Practice | Business |
|---|---|---|
| IP ownership | Practice owner's IP is the product | Business may use others' IP or methods |
| Face of the brand | Practice owner is the face | Owner may step back; others deliver |
| Team | Small support team (admin, sales) — they don't do what you do | Team delivers the core product/service |
| Scale path | Certification, licensing, franchising | Hire, delegate, systematise |
| Example | Jess (TLI) — she is the coach | Marshall Goldsmith — certified coaches deliver under his method |

The contractor trap: having an ABN and doing daily-rate contractor roles is not a practice. It is a step between permanent employment and a practice — but it is not the destination. The distinction matters because the design of the practice (positioning, offerings, content, pricing) is completely different from the design of a contractor career.

**Soundbite:**
> "A practice is built on your IP and you are the face. The moment you step back and let others do what you do, you've moved from a practice to a business. Both are valid — but they require completely different designs."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Ask: "Am I building a practice or a business?" If the answer is a practice, then everything — positioning, content, pricing, offerings — must be built around you as the IP and the face.
2. If the goal is eventually to scale to a business (certification, franchising), design the practice first. The business comes after the practice is established.
3. Avoid the contractor trap: if all engagements are daily-rate roles where you are a resource on someone else's plan, you are not building a practice. You are doing contracting with extra steps.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The practice-to-business transition is possible (Marshall Goldsmith is the example), but it requires a codified method, a certification pathway, and a brand that is strong enough to survive the practice owner stepping back.
- Most professionals who say they want a "business" actually want a "practice" — they want to be the face, do the work, and be paid for their IP. The distinction matters because the design is different.
- The practice is not a limitation on scale — it is the foundation for it. You cannot franchise what you have not yet codified.

**Domain:**
Positioning — specifically, the foundational clarity about what is being built. Also touches Potential (the practice-to-business transition is the ceiling-breaking trajectory).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Menu Trap" from Session 3 — the menu is what happens when a practice owner tries to run a business without the practice foundations.
- Connects to the "GTL Model" — the problem solver, opportunity creator, future shaper distinction maps directly onto the practice evolution from contractor to practice owner to business owner.

**Diagram potential:**
Yes. A progression diagram: Contractor (daily rate, resource on a plan) → Practice (IP-based, practice owner is the face) → Business (certified others deliver, owner steps back). Each stage labelled with the key design requirement.

**LinkedIn post idea:**
Hook: "I thought I was building a business. I was actually building a practice. Here's why that distinction changed everything." Story: the practice vs. business distinction — what it means for design, pricing, and scale. CTA: "Are you building a practice or a business? The design is completely different."

## 2. The Three Lenses of Positioning — You, the Work, and Them

**Source:** Session 4, approx. 38:23–73:30 | Vimeo: 2025-12-11

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "The three components of positioning — three lenses. You, as in what are your values, what do you genuinely care about, how do you show up. Two is the work itself — what outcomes do you help deliver, how do you work, what is your method in plain language, and what is the edge. Three is them — why should clients care about what you do?"
> "This is like the three-legged stool. They all need to be there and they all need to be at the same level, otherwise the chair is going to be wobbly."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Practice owners who focus entirely on the "you" lens (look at me, how great I am) or the "work" lens (here is my method and all my knowledge) and completely miss the "them" lens (why should the client care?). The result is content and positioning that is intellectually impressive but commercially inert.

**Value add:**
The three-lens framework is the most complete and practical positioning model for practice owners in the change/transformation/advisory space. It gives a clear diagnostic for why positioning is not working (which lens is missing or weak?) and a clear design framework for building positioning that converts.

**The framework:**
**Lens 1 — You:**
- What are your values and what do you genuinely care about?
- How do you show up? What is the impression people get when they first work with you?
- What language do people who have worked with you use to describe you?
- This is the input from the 18-month reflection exercise (Session 3) — the words, sentiments, and vibes of how people describe you from working with you.
- Key insight: the "you" lens is not about self-promotion. It is about extracting the essence of who you are from how others experience you — and then designing your positioning so that people who don't know you would say the same thing before working with you.

**Lens 2 — The Work:**
- What outcomes do you help deliver?
- What is your method — in plain English, not in jargon?
- What is the edge — the specific thing that makes your work different from everyone else in the same space?
- Jess's example: "I dropped the digital. I don't do transformation from a perspective of delivering. I do it end to end. My edge is integrated end-to-end and change adoption."
- The edge is not about credentials or years of experience. It is about the specific angle, the specific problem, the specific outcome that only you deliver in the way you deliver it.

**Lens 3 — Them:**
- Why should clients care about what you do?
- What is at stake for them if this problem is not solved?
- How does your work fit into their wider strategy?
- The "bleeding neck problem" — the problem that is so urgent, so high-stakes, that the client cannot afford not to solve it.
- The diagnostic: if you are hearing "we've tried this before," "we already have someone that does that," or "that's good in theory but not for us" — you have not honed the "them" lens.

**The content inversion rule:**
When building the practice (doing the positioning work), the order is: You → Work → Them.
When doing content (LinkedIn, articles, pitches), the order is reversed: Them → hint to the Work → give the vibe of You.
This is why most practice owners' content fails: they post in the practice-building order (you, work, them) instead of the content order (them, work, you).

**Soundbite:**
> "The three-legged stool: you, the work, and them. All three need to be there and at the same level. One leg shorter than the others and the whole thing wobbles. Most people have two strong legs and one they've never thought about."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Do the three-lens audit: for each lens, write down what you currently have. Where are the gaps?
2. For the "you" lens: extract the essence from the 18-month reflection exercise. What words do people use? What is the impression you give?
3. For the "work" lens: define your edge in two sentences. What is the specific thing you do that no one else does in the same way?
4. For the "them" lens: identify the bleeding neck problem. What is the problem so urgent that the client cannot afford not to solve it? What is at stake if it is not solved?
5. For content: always start with "them" — the problem, the pain, the stake. Then hint to the work. Then give the vibe of who you are.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The "them" lens is the hardest because it requires getting out of your own head and into the client's world. The best way to develop it is to listen — in meetings, in conversations, in LinkedIn comments — to how clients describe their own problems in their own language.
- The positioning is not a one-time exercise. It is an ongoing process of refinement. Every engagement, every conversation, every piece of content is data that refines the positioning.
- The "you" lens is not about self-promotion — it is about authenticity. The goal is not to make yourself sound impressive; it is to make yourself recognisable to the right people.

**Domain:**
Positioning — this is the core positioning framework. Also touches Practice (the three lenses shape the practice design) and Potential (the "them" lens is what enables premium pricing).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "18-Month Reflection Exercise" from Session 3 — the output of that exercise feeds directly into the "you" lens.
- Connects to the "Bleeding Neck Problem" later in this session — the bleeding neck problem is the "them" lens made concrete.
- Connects to the "Compelling Profile" work in Session 5 — the LinkedIn profile is the three-lens framework made visible.

**Diagram potential:**
Yes. A three-legged stool: each leg labelled (You, Work, Them). The seat is labelled "Positioning." A second diagram showing the inversion: "Practice Building Order" (You → Work → Them) vs. "Content Order" (Them → Work → You).

**LinkedIn post idea:**
Hook: "Most consultants' content has two strong legs and one they've never thought about. Here's the one they're missing." Story: the three-lens framework — you, the work, and them — and why the "them" lens is the one that converts. CTA: "Which lens is weakest in your positioning?"

**Reel / YouTube video idea:**

## 3. The Bleeding Neck Problem — Why Clients Buy

**Source:** Session 4, approx. 62:49–68:57 | Vimeo: 2025-12-11

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "It's a bit of a — I need to find a better term because I don't like it, but it kind of conveys the message — which is a bleeding neck problem. Somebody is bleeding from the neck. Very two minutes and they will be dead. Something is at high stake."
> "The best sales ever is when you articulate the problem better than the client so that they would say, yes, this is what I'm going through. This is it. You get me. If you've done that, you know you're already 50 percent in."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Practice owners who describe their offerings in terms of what they do (the method, the process, the framework) rather than what the client is experiencing (the problem, the pain, the stake). The result is positioning that is intellectually clear but emotionally inert — the client understands it but does not feel compelled to act.

**Value add:**
The bleeding neck problem is the most commercially powerful framing in practice positioning. It shifts the conversation from "here is what I do" to "here is what you are experiencing and what is at stake if it is not solved." This is the difference between a pitch that gets a polite nod and a pitch that gets a "yes, that's exactly what I'm going through — how do we start?"

**The framework:**
The bleeding neck problem has three elements:
1. **The problem** — what is the specific, urgent, high-stakes problem the client is experiencing right now?
2. **The stake** — what happens if this problem is not solved? What is the cost — financial, reputational, personal?
3. **The relief** — what does the world look like when the problem is solved? What does the client get back?

Jess's example for the individual transformation leader:
- Problem: "People are starting to talk about the program in closed meetings and they're not happy with how things are going."
- Stake: "Don't go into the holidays with a butterfly in your stomach, dreading the very first Monday after the holiday."
- Relief: "You're not going to go around people saying, oh, well, people are naturally resistant to change — and you just believe what you just said."

Jess's example for the corporate sponsor:
- Problem: "Don't leave your people wondering and brewing on how they're going to resist the change because they don't understand it."
- Stake: "Going to the first board meeting saying, only now we've started realising that we're off track — which is going to be an embarrassing moment."
- Relief: "Now they're asking because they are in recovery mode, not asking because we don't know how to solve it mode."

**Key insight:** The same IP, the same offering, the same method — but completely different language, different stakes, different relief for the individual vs. the corporate. This is why the avatar decision (individual vs. corporate) matters: the bleeding neck problem is different for each, even when the solution is the same.

**Soundbite:**
> "The best sales ever is when you articulate the problem better than the client. When they say 'you get me' — you're already 50 percent in."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. For each avatar (individual, corporate), write the bleeding neck problem in three parts: the problem, the stake, the relief.
2. Test it: read it to someone who matches the avatar. Do they say "yes, that's exactly what I'm going through"? If not, refine.
3. Use the bleeding neck problem as the opening of every pitch, every LinkedIn post, every email subject line. Start with the pain — not the solution.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The bleeding neck problem is not about fear-mongering. It is about empathy — demonstrating that you understand the client's world so deeply that you can articulate their problem better than they can.
- The language of the bleeding neck problem must come from the client, not from the practice owner. Listen to how clients describe their problems in their own words — and use that language.
- The pricing follows the bleeding neck problem: if the problem is urgent enough and the stake is high enough, the price becomes a secondary consideration. "The price tag is $150k for 10 days of my time. They will be willing to pay that if I framed it the right way."

**Domain:**
Positioning — specifically, the commercial translation of the "them" lens into a compelling problem statement. Also touches Practice (the bleeding neck problem shapes the offering design) and Potential (the bleeding neck problem is what enables premium pricing).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Three Lenses of Positioning" earlier in this session — the bleeding neck problem is the "them" lens made concrete.
- Connects to the "Compelling Profile" work in Session 5 — the LinkedIn profile is built around the bleeding neck problem.

**Diagram potential:**
Yes. A three-part visual: Problem (what the client is experiencing) → Stake (what happens if not solved) → Relief (what the world looks like when solved). An arrow from left to right labelled "The bleeding neck problem."

**LinkedIn post idea:**
Hook: "The best sales conversation I ever had started with a problem. Not mine — theirs." Story: the bleeding neck problem — why articulating the client's problem better than they can is the most powerful sales tool. CTA: "What is the bleeding neck problem you solve?"

## 4. The "Look at Me" Content Trap — Why Smart People Post and Get Nothing

**Source:** Session 4, approx. 31:23–38:27 | Vimeo: 2025-12-11

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "Unfortunately, some really good people who can have very profitable good practice have spent about a year and a half posting three times a week. And all of their newsletter and all their posts have two big mistakes. One, they're talking about themselves and how great they are. Second, they are dumping too much information on people. It's like drinking from a fire hose."
> "They haven't yet dissected their intellectual property. They haven't linked it to what problems they want to solve, not everything under the sun. And as a result, their language is not yet honed in."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Professionals who invest significant time and effort into LinkedIn content — posting consistently, writing newsletters, attending events — and get zero commercial return. They are smart, credible, and genuinely expert. But their content is not converting because it is built on the wrong foundation.

**Value add:**
The "look at me" trap is the most common and most expensive mistake in practice building. Understanding it — and the two specific failure modes (self-promotion and information dumping) — allows a practice owner to immediately diagnose and fix their content strategy. The fix is not more content; it is better-positioned content.

**The framework:**
The "look at me" trap has two failure modes:
1. **Self-promotion** — "Look at me, how great I am." The content is about the practice owner's credentials, achievements, and expertise. It is written from the inside out — what I know, what I've done, what I can do. The reader's response: "Impressive, but what does this have to do with me?"
2. **Information dumping** — sharing everything the practice owner knows in their own language. The content is intellectually rich but commercially inert. The reader's response: "Wow, that's a lot. She definitely knows what she's talking about. But I don't know if it's a fit for what we're doing."

The root cause of both failure modes is the same: the practice owner has not yet dissected their IP and linked it to the specific problems they want to solve. Without that foundation, the content has no commercial spine — it is just knowledge floating in the air.

The fix: do the positioning work first (the three lenses, the bleeding neck problem, the avatar). Then build the content from the "them" lens outward.

**Soundbite:**
> "A year and a half of posting. Three times a week. Zero commercial return. Not because they're not smart — because they're posting in the wrong order. They start with themselves. The client doesn't care about you. They care about their problem."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Audit your last 10 LinkedIn posts. For each one, ask: "Does this start with the client's problem, or does it start with me?"
2. If more than 3 of the 10 start with you (your experience, your credentials, your method), you are in the "look at me" trap.
3. The fix: for every piece of content, start with the bleeding neck problem. Then hint to the work. Then give the vibe of who you are.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The "look at me" trap is seductive because it feels like the right thing to do — you are establishing credibility, demonstrating expertise, building authority. But it produces the opposite effect: when people cannot see themselves in your content, they scroll past.
- The information dumping failure mode is particularly common among professionals who are genuinely expert — because they have so much to say. The discipline is to say one thing, say it clearly, and make it about the reader.
- The algorithm also punishes the "look at me" trap: content that does not generate engagement (because readers cannot see themselves in it) gets shown to fewer people. The result is a downward spiral of effort and diminishing returns.

**Domain:**
Positioning — specifically, the content strategy that flows from the positioning. Also touches Practice (the content is the primary practice-building activity in the early stages).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Three Lenses of Positioning" earlier in this session — the content inversion rule (Them → Work → You) is the antidote to the "look at me" trap.
- Connects to the "Micro-Celebrity" concept later in this session — the micro-celebrity is what the practice owner becomes when the content is built on the right foundation.

**Diagram potential:**
Yes. A before/after visual: "Look at Me" content (starts with you, dumps information, no commercial return) vs. "Them-First" content (starts with the client's problem, hints to the work, gives the vibe of who you are, converts).

**LinkedIn post idea:**
Hook: "I spent 18 months posting on LinkedIn. Zero clients. Here's what I was doing wrong." Story: the "look at me" trap — the two failure modes and the root cause. CTA: "Does your content start with you or with your client's problem?"

## 5. The Micro-Celebrity Concept — The Goal of Practice Building

**Source:** Session 4, approx. 25:41–26:55 | Vimeo: 2025-12-11

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "When you start to become micro celebrity — people know you, you're very well known in your circle — I call this micro celebrity. When you start to get to that status of micro celebrity, you get a special — people wait to hear from you."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Practice owners who have no clear goal for their brand-building efforts — who post, attend events, and network without a clear picture of what success looks like. The micro-celebrity concept gives them a concrete, achievable goal: not global fame, but deep recognition within a specific circle.

**Value add:**
The micro-celebrity concept reframes the goal of practice building from "becoming famous" (unachievable and irrelevant) to "becoming the go-to person in your specific circle" (achievable and commercially powerful). It also gives a clear milestone: when people in your circle wait to hear from you, you have reached micro-celebrity status.

**The framework:**
The micro-celebrity has three characteristics:
1. **Circle-specific recognition** — you are not famous to everyone; you are deeply known and respected within a specific professional circle (e.g., change and transformation leaders in Australia and New Zealand).
2. **Anticipation** — people in your circle wait to hear from you. They open your emails. They read your posts. They share your content. They refer you.
3. **Systems-enabled presence** — at micro-celebrity status, you can schedule content in advance and maintain presence without being constantly "on." The systems do the work.

The path to micro-celebrity: consistent, positioned content over time. The algorithm rewards consistency. The audience rewards relevance. The commercial returns come when the audience trusts you enough to buy.

**Soundbite:**
> "Micro-celebrity: not famous to everyone — known to the right people. When they wait to hear from you, you've arrived."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Define your circle: who are the specific people you want to be known by? Be specific — not "change professionals" but "change and transformation leaders in mid-to-large organisations in Australia and New Zealand who are running complex programmes."
2. Define what micro-celebrity looks like in that circle: what would people say about you? What would they refer you for?
3. Build toward that: every piece of content, every event, every conversation is an investment in micro-celebrity within that specific circle.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The micro-celebrity concept is powerful because it makes the goal achievable. You do not need to be Brené Brown. You need to be the person that your specific circle thinks of first when they have the problem you solve.
- The algorithm punishes disappearance: Jess's experience of taking a break over Christmas and losing four months of LinkedIn momentum is the cautionary tale. Consistency is not optional.
- Micro-celebrity is not just about LinkedIn. It is about being present in the spaces where your circle gathers — events, conferences, communities, referral networks.

**Domain:**
Positioning — specifically, the brand-building goal that gives direction to the practice-building activities. Also touches Practice (the micro-celebrity is the commercial outcome of the practice-building work).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Look at Me" content trap earlier in this session — the micro-celebrity is what you become when you escape the trap.
- Connects to the "Build While You Have Income" principle from Session 3 — the micro-celebrity status takes time to build, which is why starting while employed is the strategic advantage.

**Diagram potential:**
Yes. A progression visual: Unknown → Known to some → Micro-celebrity (known to the right people, people wait to hear from you) → Business (systems-enabled, scalable). Each stage labelled with the key activity required to get there.

**LinkedIn post idea:**
Hook: "You don't need to be Brené Brown. You need to be the person your circle thinks of first." Story: the micro-celebrity concept — what it is, why it matters, and how to build toward it. CTA: "Who is the micro-celebrity in your circle? What would it take for that to be you?"

## 1. The Practice-Building Roadmap — All the Components

**Source:** Session 5, approx. 13:18–26:16 | Vimeo: 2026-01-15

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "I don't follow the plan like this, then this, then that. But we would need to have covered everything. And it depends on how you progress and there will be things that are easier to observe than others. This doesn't mean like one session, this is session two, one session, this is session three. It doesn't work like that. This is more of the thinking process of everything that we would need to offer."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Practice owners who are overwhelmed by the sheer number of things they need to do — and who either try to do everything at once (and do nothing well) or who focus only on the visible parts (LinkedIn, website) and miss the foundational work (positioning, niche, offer architecture). The roadmap gives them a complete map of the territory without prescribing a rigid sequence.

**Value add:**
The practice-building roadmap is the most complete and honest overview of what it actually takes to build a practice from scratch. It names all the components, explains why each matters, and clarifies the sequence (foundational elements first, then execution). It also normalises the non-linearity of the process — the components are not a checklist to complete in order; they are a territory to navigate.

**The framework — the full practice-building roadmap:**

| Component | What it covers | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Positioning | How you are known in the market. What people say about you when you're not in the room. | The foundation of everything. Without it, all other work is misdirected. |

The foundational elements (positioning, why/story, mindset, niche) are not supposed to change often. Once established, they are the spine of the practice. The execution elements (offer architecture, business assets, plan, marketing, sales, delivery, money) build on top of them.

You cannot have a healthy practice before you have a finalised offer and price — but you can start building toward it. The foundational work is time and energy well spent because it shapes everything that comes after.

**Soundbite:**

## 2. The Positioning Matrix — The One-Pager That Captures Everything

**Source:** Session 5, approx. 43:25–50:45 | Vimeo: 2026-01-15

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "I want to introduce to you a concept called the positioning matrix. This is going to be a one pager that captures the essence of the stuff that you've done. It's a live document so that whenever you feel like you've learned something new or something, there's a new idea or something in terms of articulating your value or something you bring to the table, or you've evolved your practice, you keep updating that positioning matrix."
> "I have like I don't know 200 versions of this positioning matrix because every time it changes it doesn't change because everything changes — it changes because you get clearer."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Practice owners who have done extensive positioning work (exercises, reflections, documents) but cannot distil it into a usable, actionable format. The positioning matrix is the tool that takes all of that work and compresses it into a single page that can be used as a reference point for all positioning decisions.

**Value add:**
The positioning matrix is the most practical tool in the GTL Programme. It is not a strategy document or a white paper — it is a one-pager that captures the essence of who you are, what you do, and who you do it for. It is the reference point for all content, all pitches, all offer design, all LinkedIn work. When in doubt, go back to the matrix.

**The framework — the positioning matrix components:**

| Component | What it captures | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| History | The relevant bits of your background that connect to what you do. | Not a CV. Just the thread that connects your past to your practice. |
| Category | The boring, overarching label for what you do. | Start with the simplest possible description. Then narrow it down. |
| Obsession | What lights you up in all the work that you've done. | Can be what lights you up while doing it, or what lights you up as a result. |
| Examples | Dot points, analogies, stories that illustrate what you do. | These will come over time. Don't force them. |
| Uniqueness | What sets you apart. Not years of experience — the specific angle, approach, or IP. | This is the edge from the three-lens framework. |
| Package ideas | Early ideas about what you might offer. Not commitments — just ideas. | These will evolve. |
| Problems | What problems do you help solve? How does what you do help clients get to where they want to be? | This is the "them" lens from the three-lens framework. |

**The category ladder:**
The category is not just one thing — it is a ladder from the most overarching (the boring, everyone-understands-it label) to the most specific (the niche). The positioning matrix captures the top of the ladder. The niche work (done later in the programme) fills in the rungs below.

Jess's example: "If I say transformation to somebody who isn't in the industry, they'd say, 'what is transformation? Like you do gym stuff?' So my category to somebody who isn't in the industry is: I manage projects. Or, for someone more advanced: whenever a company wants to change their system, that would be me."

**The evolution insight:**
The positioning matrix changes because you get clearer — not because your positioning is fundamentally different. 90% of changes to the matrix are clarity changes. 10% are genuine positioning evolutions (e.g., shifting from individuals to corporate, or from one niche to another).

**Soundbite:**
> "I have 200 versions of this positioning matrix. It doesn't change because everything changes — it changes because you get clearer. That's the difference."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Create your positioning matrix as a one-pager. Fill in each component with bullet points — not paragraphs.
2. Keep it live. Update it whenever you get clearer on any component.
3. Use it as the reference point for all positioning decisions: content, pitches, offer design, LinkedIn profile.
4. When you feel stuck or unclear on any positioning decision, go back to the matrix. The answer is usually there.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The matrix is for you, not for clients. It does not need to be polished or presentable. It just needs to be clear enough that you can refer to it two months later and know what you meant.
- The obsession component is the most underrated. What lights you up is not just a personal preference — it is a commercial signal. The work you are most excited about is the work you do best, and the work you do best is the work that commands the highest price.
- The category ladder is a practical tool for every conversation. You do not describe yourself the same way to a grocery shop owner as you do to a chief transformation officer. The ladder gives you the language for every context.

**Source:** Session 5, approx. 10:26–43:07 and 50:51–64:47 | Vimeo: 2026-01-15

**Pain point this addresses:**

**Value add:**
The why and story is not a marketing tool (though it becomes one). It is first and foremost a personal anchor — the thing that keeps you going when everything else is telling you to stop. The commercial value is secondary: people gravitate toward practice owners who have a clear story because it makes them different, memorable, and trustworthy.

**The framework:**
The why and story has two purposes:
1. **Personal anchor** — the thing that grounds you when times are hard. When the bad client disputes your charges, when the troll attacks you on LinkedIn, when the self-doubt hits at 2am — the why is what brings you back to yourself.
2. **Commercial differentiator** — the thing that makes you different from every other practice owner in your space. People gravitate toward those who have a clear story because it signals authenticity, conviction, and purpose.

The story is not about the practice. It is not "I want to help change professionals build better practices." The shining light is the metaphor for the inner state of the practice owner. In corporate, you do good work and then feel good (external reward → internal state). In practice, it is the reverse: you feel good first, and then you do good work (internal state → external output). The why and story is the tool for getting back to the light when it dims.

4. When you hit a hard moment in the practice, go back to the story. Not to the business plan, not to the revenue targets — to the story.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The story does not need to be dramatic or traumatic. It just needs to be true. The test is: when you tell it without anyone watching, does it feel like you? Or does it feel like you're performing?
- The story will evolve. The first version is rarely the final version. But you need a first version to work from.
- The commercial value of the story is real but secondary. Do not write the story for the market. Write it for yourself. The market will respond to the authenticity.

## 4. The Evolution of Lead Generation — Push, Pull, Value and Identity Attraction

**Source:** Session 5, approx. 51:46–61:00 | Vimeo: 2026-01-15

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "Back in the days, maybe 10 years ago or seven years ago, people who've got the practice and business — they do marketing, they fill out their back line, they get prospects, they attempt sales, they do conversion. This is the common system in corporate. We've moved from that now."
> "The one that is more niche, but you can't start from there. But this is what I want to seed for you as we're doing pull system. Once you have that honed in — this is now the space that I want to play in. I'm already there, but I'm not fully there yet — which is the value and identity attraction. People get attracted to you because of what you say, not what you offer."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Practice owners who are stuck in the push system (marketing → leads → sales → conversion) and wondering why it is so exhausting and inefficient. The evolution of lead generation framework gives them a map of where they are, where they are going, and why the pull system and value and identity attraction are the destination — not the starting point.

**Value add:**
The three-level evolution of lead generation is one of the most strategically important frameworks in the GTL Programme. It explains why the practice-building work (positioning, story, niche, offer architecture) is not just foundational — it is the mechanism by which you move from the push system to the pull system to the value and identity attraction. The work is not just about getting clients; it is about changing the nature of how clients come to you.

**The framework — the three levels:**

**Level 1 — Push System (the common corporate system):**
- Marketing → leads/prospects → sales → conversion → clients → relationship building
- You are pushing your way to clients. You are doing the work of finding them, convincing them, closing them.
- This is the starting point for most practice owners. It is valid, but it is exhausting and inefficient at scale.
- Characteristics: high effort, low leverage, price-sensitive, requires constant activity.

**Level 2 — Pull System (positioning, referrals, recommendations):**
- Positioning → referrals → recommendations/testimonials → clients come to you
- You are not pushing people to buy from you. They are buying from you because of your positioning, your reputation, your referrals.
- This is what Jess has been doing for the last three years at least.
- Characteristics: medium effort, high leverage, less price-sensitive, builds on itself over time.
- The right level for a solo practice in change and transformation that is building from scratch.

**Level 3 — Value and Identity Attraction (the destination):**
- People are attracted to you because of what you say, not what you offer.
- They follow you because of your opinion, the way you talk about things, the way you get people.
- When you make an offer, people buy it straight away — no discount codes, no 50% off, no convincing.
- Characteristics: low effort (the value and identity is the marketing), very high leverage, completely price-insensitive, requires a strong and established positioning.
- You cannot start from here. You get here after years of Level 1 and Level 2 work.

**The Brené Brown example:**
> "I follow Brown, I follow — that's where she is. They follow them because of their opinion, the way they talk about things. You don't need to buy from them. You will eventually, but you will say things like, 'you get me, the language that you're using, I feel it in my heart.' That's when you know you're at that level."

**The key insight for practice owners who are just starting:**
Start with Level 1 (you need to do some push). Move quickly to Level 2 (positioning, referrals, testimonials). Seed for Level 3 (build the positioning and story that will eventually attract people to you because of who you are, not what you sell). Do not try to start at Level 3 — it does not work without the foundation.

**Soundbite:**
> "People get attracted to you because of what you say, not what you offer. That's Level 3. You can't start there. But you can build toward it. And when you get there, they're waiting for you to say, 'I have this.' No discount codes. No convincing. They just buy."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Identify which level you are currently at. Most practice owners starting out are at Level 1.
2. For Level 2: focus on positioning (the three lenses), referrals (keep good relationships with those who know you), and testimonials (get them early, even at a discount or for free).
3. For Level 3: build the positioning and story that will eventually attract people to you because of who you are. This is a long-term investment — the content, the newsletter, the community, the book.
4. Do not skip levels. The dependencies are real: you cannot build Level 2 without Level 1 foundations; you cannot build Level 3 without Level 2 foundations.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The level you are at determines the language you use in your marketing. Level 1 is "here is what I offer." Level 2 is "here is what others say about me." Level 3 is "here is what I think" — and people buy because they trust your thinking.
- The transition from Level 1 to Level 2 is the most important transition in practice building. It is the transition from "I am selling" to "I am being bought." The positioning work is what enables this transition.
- Some practice owners stay at Level 1 forever because their offers are low-ticket and transactional (e.g., courses up to $250). That is valid — but it means the practice will always require high effort to maintain. Level 2 and Level 3 are what enable the lifestyle practice.

**Domain:**
Practice — specifically, the marketing and sales evolution. Also touches Positioning (the positioning work is what enables the transition from Level 1 to Level 2) and Potential (Level 3 is the ceiling-breaking destination).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Micro-Celebrity" concept from Session 4 — the micro-celebrity is the Level 2/Level 3 transition point.
- Connects to the "Practice-Building Roadmap" earlier in this session — the marketing systems and sales effectiveness components of the roadmap.
- Connects to the "Why and Story" earlier in this session — the story is the foundation of the value and identity that attracts at Level 3.

**Diagram potential:**
Yes. A three-level pyramid: Level 1 (Push — marketing, leads, sales, conversion) at the base; Level 2 (Pull — positioning, referrals, recommendations) in the middle; Level 3 (Value and Identity Attraction — people attracted by what you say, not what you offer) at the top. An arrow showing the progression from base to top.

## 2. The Five Positioning Roles — Doing, Hiring, Advising, Leading, Coaching/Mentoring

**Source:** Session 6, approx. 23:27–33:34 | Vimeo: 2026-02-18

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "Positioning — are you gonna be doing, hiring, advising, leading, coaching/mentoring? And it could be a combination. But what is the main thing? What are they expecting you to do?"
> "Each one of these is a different structure, different language, different price, different engagement. So we need to be clear on that."
> "No advisor or leader — like leader from a business practice point of view — works full-time."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Practice owners (and experienced professionals entering new client engagements) who go into a meeting, hear a description of the work, and immediately start thinking about what they would do — without first clarifying what role the client is actually expecting them to play. The result is a proposal that answers the wrong question, at the wrong price, with the wrong structure.

**Value add:**
The five positioning roles framework is one of the most practically useful tools in the GTL Programme for practice owners who are doing client work. It gives a clear taxonomy of the different ways a practice owner can engage with a client — and makes explicit that each role has a completely different structure, language, price, and engagement model. Confusing the roles (or letting the client confuse them) is the most common source of scope creep, underpricing, and burnout.

**The framework — the five positioning roles:**

| Role | What it means | Typical structure | Price point | Time commitment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Doing | Hands-on execution — you are doing the work yourself | Short-term (max 3 months), high-intensity | Most expensive | 3+ days/week |
| Hiring | Building the capability — you help recruit, structure, and set up the team, then leave | Project-based | High | Variable |
| Advising | Advisory retainer — you advise the person who leads | Retainer (e.g., 3 days/month) | Very high per day (e.g., $20k/day) | 3 days/month |
| Leading | You are running the programme — this is your programme | Minimum 3 days/week | High | 3+ days/week |
| Coaching/Mentoring | You work with the CEO and C-level only | Ongoing, lower intensity | High per session | Variable |

**The key insight:**
The "doing" role is the most expensive — not the cheapest. This is counterintuitive for most practice owners who think of "doing" as the commodity role. But when a practice owner does the hands-on work, they are bringing their full expertise, their judgment, and their reputation to the execution. That is not a commodity — it is the most valuable thing they can offer, and it should be priced accordingly.

Jess's example:
> "My doing, for example, when I do hands-on, it's for programs that are about to blow up and we need to bring them back on track before the next board meeting. So it has to be attached to something that — okay, whatever, it's a blank check. Just help us. And that would be $100,000 to bring you back on track."

**The evolution insight:**
The roles can change over the course of an engagement. A typical progression for a new client with no internal capability:
1. **Doing** (first 3 months) — hands-on, high-intensity, most expensive. You are building the foundation.
2. **Leading** (months 4–12) — you are running the programme, but the team is now doing more of the execution.
3. **Advising** (ongoing) — you step back to a retainer model. The team runs the programme; you advise.

This progression is not just commercially smart — it is also the right thing for the client. The goal is always to build internal capability, not to create dependency.

**Soundbite:**
> "Each of these roles is a different structure, different language, different price, different engagement. If you don't know which one you're in, you're in the wrong one."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Before any client engagement, map the five roles and ask: which one is this client expecting? Which one do I want to be in?
2. If the client's expectation and your preference are different, have the conversation explicitly. Do not assume they will figure it out.
3. Design the engagement to evolve: start in the role that makes sense for the client's current state, and plan the transition to the next role.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The most common mistake is conflating "leading" with "doing." Leading means the programme is yours — the strategy, the roadmap, the decisions. Doing means you are executing. They are very different, and they should be priced very differently.
- The "advising" role is the most leveraged — 3 days a month at $20k/day is $60k/month. But it requires a client who is mature enough to use an advisor effectively. A client with no internal capability cannot use an advisor.
- The "coaching/mentoring" role is the most personal — you are working with the CEO and C-level only. It is the highest-leverage role in terms of impact, but it requires the deepest trust.

**Domain:**
Practice — specifically, the offer architecture and sales effectiveness components of the practice-building roadmap. Also touches Positioning (the role you play in a client engagement is a positioning decision, not just a commercial one).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Practice-Building Roadmap" from Session 5 — the five positioning roles are the offer architecture component made concrete.
- Connects to the "Bleeding Neck Problem" from Session 4 — the role you play determines the price, and the price is justified by the bleeding neck problem.

## 3. The Integration-Optimization-Transformation Roadmap for M&A/PE Companies

**Source:** Session 6, approx. 33:34–35:10 | Vimeo: 2026-02-18

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "What you're talking about doesn't seem like a transformation program. It seems like an integration program, mainly."
> "Transformation is — usually when there is an M&A, we don't start with transformation. We need to integrate them first. So you integrate, then you'd move to optimization. Which can lead to some cost reductions, operational efficiencies, normalizing, streamlining, process, org chart, job titles, pay. And then you need to stabilize the system, and then we transform. You can transform right after M&A — disaster."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Clients (and practice owners) who use "transformation" as a catch-all term for any significant change — and as a result, design the wrong programme, in the wrong sequence, at the wrong pace. The integration-optimization-transformation roadmap gives a clear, sequenced framework for M&A and PE-backed company engagements.

**Value add:**
This is one of the most commercially specific frameworks in the GTL Programme. It is directly applicable to any M&A or PE-backed company engagement — which is a significant and growing market. The framework also demonstrates Jess's depth of expertise: she can immediately diagnose the difference between integration, optimization, and transformation, and she knows why the sequence matters.

**The framework — the three-stage roadmap:**

| Stage | What it covers | Typical activities | When it applies |
|---|---|---|---|
| Integration | Bringing the acquired companies together | Operational streamlining, org chart alignment, process harmonisation, systems integration, culture alignment | Immediately after M&A |
| Optimization | Improving efficiency and performance of the integrated entity | Cost reductions, operational efficiencies, org chart refinement, job title/pay normalisation, process optimisation | After integration is stable |
| Transformation | Fundamentally changing the nature of the business | New business models, new capabilities, new culture, new market positioning | After optimization is stable |

**The key insight:**
The sequence is not optional — it is structural. Trying to transform before you have integrated is a disaster. The people are still in shock from the acquisition; the systems are not aligned; the culture is fractured. Transformation requires a stable platform. Integration builds the platform. Optimization strengthens it. Transformation builds on it.

**The PE-specific nuance:**
PE companies have a financial timeline (typically 3–5 years to exit). This timeline shapes the roadmap: integration and optimization must happen fast enough to deliver the EBITDA growth that justifies the exit valuation. Transformation is the longer-term play — it is what makes the company worth more at exit.

**Soundbite:**
> "You can transform right after M&A — disaster. Integrate first. Optimize. Stabilize. Then transform. The sequence is not optional."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. In any M&A or PE-backed company engagement, start by diagnosing which stage the company is in: integration, optimization, or transformation.
2. Use the client's own language to describe the stage: if they say "integration," use "integration." If they say "transformation," gently reframe to the correct stage.
3. Design the roadmap to show the progression: integration → optimization → transformation. This gives the client a clear picture of the journey and positions you as the guide for the whole journey, not just the first stage.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The integration stage is the most underestimated. Companies consistently underestimate how long it takes to integrate — and how much disruption it causes. The practice owner who can manage integration well is the most valuable person in the room.
- The optimization stage is where the quick wins are. Cost reductions, operational efficiencies, process improvements — these are the things that PE companies care about most in the short term. Delivering quick wins in the optimization stage builds the trust and the budget for the transformation stage.
- The transformation stage is where the real value is created. But it requires patience — and a client who is willing to invest in the long term.

**Domain:**
Practice — specifically, the offer architecture component for M&A/PE-backed company engagements. Also touches Positioning (the integration-optimization-transformation roadmap is a positioning tool — it demonstrates expertise and frames the engagement correctly).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Five Positioning Roles" earlier in this session — the role you play changes at each stage of the roadmap.
- Connects to the "Bleeding Neck Problem" from Session 4 — the bleeding neck problem for M&A/PE companies is the integration stage: if they do not integrate well, the whole investment is at risk.

**Diagram potential:**
Yes. A three-stage roadmap: Integration → Optimization → Transformation. Each stage labelled with the typical activities, the typical duration, and the typical commercial outcome. An arrow showing the progression from left to right.

Or are you trying to transform before you've integrated?"

## 4. The Scope-First Principle — Why You Cannot Write a Proposal Without Clarity

**Source:** Session 6, approx. 19:51–44:00 | Vimeo: 2026-02-18

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "The bit that I am not clear about and probably the most important thing at the moment is the scope. What you've received so far is not enough. It's not enough to create a proposal because you can assume things. You can risk making it bigger than what it is."
> "You get that, you can do a proposal. You don't have that, you don't do a proposal."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Practice owners who rush to write a proposal after the first meeting — because they are excited about the opportunity, because they want to demonstrate responsiveness, or because they do not know what else to do. The result is a proposal that answers the wrong question, at the wrong price, with the wrong structure.

**Value add:**
The scope-first principle is one of the most commercially important principles in the GTL Programme. It is the discipline of not writing a proposal until you have the four pieces of information you need: scope, positioning (role), success metrics, and support. Without these four things, any proposal is a guess — and guesses are expensive.

**The framework — the four things you need before writing a proposal:**

| What you need | Why you need it | Key questions |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Without scope, you cannot structure the engagement correctly | What is the timeline? What is the nature of the work — integration, optimization, transformation? How long? |
| Positioning (Role) | Without knowing the role, you cannot price or structure the engagement | How do you see my role? What would I be doing day in, day out? Who are the stakeholders I'll be talking to? |
| Success metrics | Without success metrics, you cannot design the engagement to succeed | What does success look like for the PE? For the CEO? For the organisation? Where do you see the organisation in 12 months? |
| Support | Without knowing the support structure, you cannot assess whether the engagement is set up to succeed | What kind of support do you have for that kind of work? What budget have you put on for the integration/optimization/transformation effort? |

**The "set up to fail" diagnostic:**
If the client's answer to the support question reveals that they expect one person (you) to do everything — no project managers, no business analysts, no embedded team — that is a set up to fail. The right response is not to refuse the engagement, but to reframe the support structure as part of the proposal: "I can help you with this, but this is what success looks like to me — and this is what we would need to get there."

**The "never send a proposal and wait" rule:**
> "Never send a proposal and just wait for a reply because when you follow up, it will sound demanding and nagging. If you just wait and do nothing, they might be — read it and they're like, our people can do this. Come on. We can just save the dollars."
> "In the very, very big deals that I go to, I don't even send a proposal. What I do is I have the proposal and I discuss it in the meeting. Once I'm done with the meeting, I'll send them the PDF."

**The three-meeting sequence:**
1. **Meet and greet** — listen, learn, build rapport. Do not ask too many questions. Do not pitch. Let the information sink in.
2. **Discovery/alignment call** — ask the four questions. Clarify scope, role, success, support. This is the meeting where you demonstrate credibility by asking the right questions.
3. **Proposal conversation** — present the proposal in a meeting, not by email. Send the PDF after the meeting, not before.

**Soundbite:**
> "You get that, you can do a proposal. You don't have that, you don't do a proposal. It's that simple."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. After every first meeting with a potential client, assess: do I have the four things I need? Scope, role, success metrics, support?
2. If not, schedule a discovery/alignment call before writing the proposal.
3. In the discovery/alignment call, use the four questions as a framework. Do not ask them all at once — weave them into a conversation.
4. When the proposal is ready, schedule a meeting to discuss it. Do not send it by email and wait.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The discovery/alignment call is not a second pitch — it is a service. You are making sure that what you propose is actually what the client needs. This is a demonstration of expertise, not a delay.
- The "never send a proposal and wait" rule applies especially to large engagements. For small engagements (a single workshop, a short coaching programme), sending by email is fine.
- The proposal conversation is where the real negotiation happens. The written proposal is just the starting point. The conversation is where you can address objections, reframe the scope, and build the relationship.

**Domain:**
Practice — specifically, the sales effectiveness component of the practice-building roadmap. Also touches Positioning (the discovery/alignment call is a positioning exercise — it demonstrates that you understand the client's world and that you are not just another consultant).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Five Positioning Roles" earlier in this session — the discovery/alignment call is where you clarify which role applies.
- Connects to the "Integration-Optimization-Transformation Roadmap" earlier in this session — the scope question in the discovery call is where you diagnose the stage.
- Connects to the "Bleeding Neck Problem" from Session 4 — the discovery call is where you identify the bleeding neck problem for this specific client.

**Diagram potential:**
Yes. A three-meeting sequence visual: Meet and Greet → Discovery/Alignment Call → Proposal Conversation. Each stage labelled with the purpose, the key activities, and the key questions.

## 5. The Budget Reframe — How to Change a Client's Mind About Investment

**Source:** Session 6, approx. 39:05–50:01 | Vimeo: 2026-02-18

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "They said, well, all we have is 100K. That's it. And I went and proposed something, and I said, by the way, I can help you with this, but this is what success looks like to me. I'm gonna exit by that time, but you're still gonna have those issues going da, da, da, possibly what's gonna happen is da, da, da, da. If you're okay with that, we can proceed. Said, okay, so what do you think we should be spending then? I said, for me, it's that much, but I would need to hire somebody who would be embedded in your teams that I will hire with you and train and be my shadow and hand over when I leave so you don't lose that. Well, that ended up being half a million."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Practice owners who accept the client's initial budget as a constraint — and as a result, either underdeliver (because the budget is not enough to do the work properly) or underprice themselves (because they adjust their scope to fit the budget rather than adjusting the budget to fit the scope).

**Value add:**
The budget reframe is the most commercially powerful technique in the GTL Programme for client engagement. It is not about negotiating — it is about helping the client understand the true cost of the problem they are trying to solve, and the true investment required to solve it properly. When done well, it is not a sales technique; it is a service.

**The framework — the budget reframe:**
1. **Accept the initial budget as a starting point**, not a constraint. "I can help you with this for 100K."
2. **Show what 100K gets them** — and what it does not get them. "By the time I exit, you will still have these issues."
3. **Ask the question** — "If you're okay with that, we can proceed. But what do you think we should be spending?"
4. **Propose the full solution** — with the rationale for the investment. "For me, it's X, but I would need to hire someone who would be embedded in your teams."
5. **Show the return** — "That ended up being half a million." The investment is justified by the outcome.

**Soundbite:**
> "They said 100K. I showed them what 100K gets. They said, what should we be spending? I said, half a million. They agreed."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. When a client gives you a budget, accept it as a starting point. Do not reject it or immediately propose something bigger.
2. Show what the budget gets — and what it does not get. Be specific about the gaps.
3. Ask the question: "If you're okay with that, we can proceed. But what do you think we should be spending?"
4. Let the client answer. They will often surprise you.
5. Propose the full solution with the rationale. The investment is justified by the outcome.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The budget reframe only works if you have done the scope-first work. Without scope clarity, you cannot show what the budget gets and what it does not get.
- The budget reframe is not about upselling — it is about alignment. The goal is to make sure that the client's investment matches the scope of the problem they are trying to solve.
- The budget reframe requires confidence. You need to be willing to say: "If you're okay with that outcome, we can proceed." Most practice owners are not willing to say this — and as a result, they accept budgets that set them up to fail.

**Domain:**
Practice — specifically, the sales effectiveness and offer architecture components of the practice-building roadmap. Also touches Potential (the budget reframe is what enables premium pricing).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Scope-First Principle" earlier in this session — the budget reframe is only possible after scope clarity.
- Connects to the "Bleeding Neck Problem" from Session 4 — the budget reframe works because the client can see the bleeding neck problem clearly.

**LinkedIn post idea:**
Hook: "A client told me they had 100K. I walked out with half a million. Here's exactly how." Story: the budget reframe — how to help a client understand the true investment required to solve the problem they are trying to solve. CTA: "Are you accepting your clients' initial budgets as constraints? Or as starting points?"

## 1. The GTL Roadmap — The Three Pillars: Positioning, Practice, Potential

**Source:** Session 7, approx. 04:15–19:05 | Onboarding session

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "Positioning, practice and potential. Now, what is positioning? Positioning is how, for a very simple term, how people perceive you."
> "If I was to assign a weight to each one, they're all equal. However, the one with the lowest score is the one that's going to be dominant. It's the one that's going to put a ceiling on the other two."
> "A lot of programs and communities out there focus solely on practice. But that misses the point."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Leaders and practice owners who are investing heavily in one area (usually practice — skills, certifications, technical knowledge) while neglecting the other two (positioning and potential). The result is the skyscraper syndrome: one tall building surrounded by houses. The ceiling on growth is always the lowest-scoring pillar.

**Value add:**
The three-pillar GTL roadmap is the master framework for everything that happens in the programme. It is the map of the territory — and it is the first thing every new member needs to understand before they can make sense of the courses, the community, and the coaching. The key insight is the ceiling principle: you are only as strong as your weakest pillar.

**The framework — the three pillars:**

| Pillar | What it covers | The most common gap |
|---|---|---|
| Positioning | How people perceive you — on LinkedIn, in meetings, in the market. How you speak about yourself. | Most leaders have weak positioning: either a "menu" (everything they've ever done), or a positioning that hasn't shifted from corporate to practice. |
| Practice | The way you do work — mindset, influence, technical know-how, thought leadership. | Most leaders have strong technical practice but lack the influence and thought leadership components. |
| Potential | The intentionality and roadmap to grow — playing to strengths, building confidence, becoming a trusted advisor. | Most leaders are not investing in their potential deliberately. They are waiting for problems to activate them. |

**The ceiling principle:**
> "If you have an amazing practice and a great potential to grow, but your positioning — you're the world's best kept secret — then so what?"

The ceiling principle is the most important insight in the GTL roadmap. It explains why technically brilliant leaders get passed over, why experienced practitioners struggle to get clients, and why the most capable people in the room are often not the most influential. The ceiling is always the weakest pillar.

**The skyscraper syndrome:**
> "You've got a very tall, sophisticated building in a city. And then everything else is houses, houses, houses. Two-story, one-story houses. They have amazing practice, but then everything else is kind of there, not there. But for you to reach and break that ceiling, you need to have many tall buildings."

The skyscraper syndrome is the visual metaphor for the ceiling principle. It is the image of a person who has invested everything in one pillar — and as a result, has a very tall building (practice) surrounded by houses (positioning, potential). The goal of the GTL programme is to build all three pillars to equal height.

**Soundbite:**
> "The one with the lowest score is the one that's going to be dominant. It's the one that's going to put a ceiling on the other two. You can't break through a ceiling you can't see."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Map yourself against the three pillars. Where are you strongest? Where are you weakest?
2. Identify the ceiling: which pillar is putting a ceiling on the other two?
3. Focus the next 90 days on the weakest pillar — not the strongest. The strongest pillar does not need your attention right now.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The three pillars are not independent — they are interdependent. Improving positioning will reveal gaps in practice. Improving potential will reveal gaps in positioning. They grow together.
- The most common mistake is investing in the strongest pillar (usually practice) because it feels good and produces visible results. The weakest pillar is where the leverage is.
- The ceiling principle applies at every stage of career and practice maturity. The same three pillars apply whether you are just starting or making $1 million a year — the depth and tactics change, but the pillars remain.

This is the master framework for the entire GTL Programme. All other IP maps onto one or more of the three pillars.

## 2. The Safe Pair of Hands Ceiling — Why Being Reliable Keeps You Stuck

**Source:** Session 7, approx. 20:01–22:16 | Onboarding session

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "I am very, very good at what I do. And the thing is, is that I can do a lot of things. And then so I'm a safe pair of hands. So people keep me there because I am a safe pair of hands. But then I cannot step up. And I know I can. But I cannot step up because people keep me in the box where I am making them safe."
> "When people say you're a safe pair of hands, I like it and I don't like it at the same time. It's obviously a praise, like it's a good thing to be a safe pair of hands. But it also dismisses your ability to lead."
> "It's like the difference between a nurse and a doctor. A nurse would be close to the work, the patient, and they would be a safe pair of hands, better be. But they're still the nurse. When something goes wrong, they don't turn to the nurse. They turn to the doctor."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Leaders who are highly competent, highly reliable, and highly valued — but who are stuck. They are the person everyone calls when something needs to be done well. They are trusted, respected, and appreciated. And they cannot move forward because their positioning has locked them into the "safe pair of hands" role.

**Value add:**
The "safe pair of hands" ceiling is one of the most powerful and resonant concepts in the GTL Programme. It names a phenomenon that many experienced leaders feel but cannot articulate. The insight is that being a safe pair of hands is not just a positioning problem — it is a self-reinforcing trap. The more reliably you deliver, the more people depend on you for delivery. The more people depend on you for delivery, the harder it is to move into the leadership and advisory roles.

**The nurse-doctor metaphor:**
The nurse-doctor metaphor is the most memorable illustration of the safe pair of hands ceiling. The nurse is close to the work, highly competent, and a safe pair of hands. But when something goes wrong, people turn to the doctor — not because the nurse is less capable, but because the doctor has a different positioning. The doctor is the decision-maker, the authority, the person who takes responsibility for the outcome.

The positioning shift from nurse to doctor is not about capability — it is about how you show up, how you speak about yourself, and what role you claim in the room.

**Jess's diagnosis:**
> "I love that you've mentioned that because everything you just said is all positioning. It's all positioning."

The safe pair of hands ceiling is a positioning problem, not a capability problem. Veronique is not stuck because she is not capable enough. She is stuck because her positioning — how she shows up, how she speaks about herself, what role she claims — has locked her into the delivery role.

**Soundbite:**
> "A safe pair of hands is a praise. But it also dismisses your ability to lead. When something goes wrong, they don't turn to the nurse. They turn to the doctor."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Identify the "safe pair of hands" signals in your current positioning: are you the person people call when something needs to be done? Or the person people call when they need to decide what to do?
2. The shift from "safe pair of hands" to "trusted advisor" is a positioning shift, not a capability shift. You do not need to become more capable — you need to show up differently.
3. The first step is to stop volunteering for the delivery role. When someone brings you a problem, resist the impulse to solve it immediately. Ask questions first.

## 3. The Problem Solver Conditioning — Why Corporate Trains You to Stay Small

**Source:** Session 7, approx. 30:03–36:21 | Onboarding session

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "The conditioning in corporate is your value is attached to what have you done. Show me what's having fun."
> "Because we don't know what are the other levels, we're stuck into that one level of the production of outputs because we are incentivized in corporate to do that."
> "Shift that to: I've just had a conversation that unlocked the mindset of somebody and made them feel good about what they do. And as soon as the brain — but the brain won't realize until you yourself become present to the moment."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Leaders who have been trained by corporate environments to measure their value by their outputs — the documents, the presentations, the deliverables, the plans. When they move into advisory or leadership roles (or start a practice), they continue to produce outputs as proof of value — and as a result, they stay in the delivery role.

**Value add:**
The problem solver conditioning is one of the most important concepts in the GTL Programme for leaders who are transitioning from corporate to practice. It names the invisible force that keeps them in the delivery role — not lack of capability, not lack of ambition, but a deep conditioning that equates value with visible output.

**The two modes:**
1. **Problem solver mode** — you see a problem, you solve it, you produce an output. The output is the proof of your value. This is the corporate default.
2. **Presence mode** — you see a problem, you ask questions, you help the person find their own answer. The conversation is the value. This is the leadership/advisory default.

The shift from problem solver mode to presence mode is not a skill change — it is a conditioning change. And conditioning changes are slow, uncomfortable, and require deliberate practice.

Veronique's breakthrough example:
> "There were two things. One is I realized when I was jumping into problem solving mode. And at the same time, I had one of the letters saying, oh my god, Veronique, that was so helpful, thank you so much. And I was like, I haven't produced anything. It was just a conversation."

This is the moment of insight: the conversation was more valuable than any output she could have produced. And the conditioning — "I haven't produced anything" — was the thing that almost prevented her from having the conversation.

**Soundbite:**
> "The conditioning in corporate is your value is attached to what have you done. Show me the output. But the most valuable thing you can do is have a conversation that unlocks someone's thinking. And there's no document for that."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Notice when you are in problem solver mode. The signal is: you are already thinking about the output before you have fully understood the problem.
2. Practice the pause. When someone brings you a problem, resist the impulse to solve it immediately. Ask one question first.
3. After every interaction, ask yourself: did I produce an output, or did I have a conversation? Which one was more valuable?

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The problem solver conditioning is not a flaw — it is a feature that served you well in corporate. The goal is not to eliminate it, but to have conscious control over when you use it.
- The shift from problem solver to presence is gradual. You will not make it in one session. But every time you notice the conditioning and choose differently, you are building the new pattern.
- The most powerful signal that you are in presence mode: the other person says "that was so helpful" — and you did not produce a single document.

**Domain:**
Potential — specifically, the mindset and conditioning component. Also touches Practice (the shift from output-driven to presence-driven is a practice shift) and Positioning (the problem solver conditioning is what keeps leaders in the "safe pair of hands" positioning).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Safe Pair of Hands Ceiling" earlier in this session — the problem solver conditioning is the root cause of the safe pair of hands ceiling.
- Connects to the "Presence Over Output" in the next section — the presence mode is the antidote to the problem solver conditioning.

**LinkedIn post idea:**
Hook: "I spent 20 years being rewarded for producing outputs. Here's what that training cost me." ## 4. Presence Over Output — The Dials Metaphor and the Doctor-Patient Analogy

**Source:** Session 7, approx. 36:21–43:01 | Onboarding session

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "That's with presence. Being present in the moment. And you are the one in control of your dials, not your conditioning and subconscious programming."
> "When do I dial up on problem solving? Because we're in crisis mode. And when do I dial down and dial up on having a feel good — it's not a fluffy conversation, but feel good as in being grounded. There is hope, there is clarity and a mindset shift."
> "It's like an anxious doctor sitting in front of a patient. He's like, I feel this and I feel this and I feel this. It's like, I know, I know I'll give you the antibiotic. But what if you just wait, wait on the antibiotic bit, wait, listen to them and then say, I think I'm going to ask you a few questions."

**Pain point this addresses:**
Leaders who know they need to shift from output-driven to presence-driven — but do not know how. The dials metaphor gives them a practical, embodied way to think about the shift. The doctor-patient analogy gives them a concrete example of what presence looks like in practice.

**Value add:**
The dials metaphor and the doctor-patient analogy are the most practical tools in Session 7 for making the shift from problem solver mode to presence mode. They are not frameworks or processes — they are images that help the leader feel the difference between the two modes, and give them a way to consciously choose which mode to be in.

**The dials metaphor:**
> "You are in control of your dials. When do I dial up on problem solving? Because we're in crisis mode. And when do I dial down and dial up on having a feel good — it's not a fluffy conversation, but feel good as in being grounded."

The dials metaphor reframes the shift from problem solver to presence as a choice — not a transformation. You are not becoming a different person. You are choosing which dial to turn up and which to turn down, based on what the situation actually needs.

The two dials:
- **Problem solving dial** — turn up in crisis mode, when there is a genuine need for immediate output.
- **Presence dial** — turn up when the person needs to be grounded, when they need clarity, when they need a mindset shift.

The key insight: most leaders have the problem solving dial permanently turned up. The goal is not to turn it off — it is to have conscious control over when you turn it up.

**The doctor-patient analogy:**
> "It's like an anxious doctor sitting in front of a patient. He's like, I feel this and I feel this and I feel this. It's like, I know, I know I'll give you the antibiotic. But what if you just wait, wait on the antibiotic bit, wait, listen to them and then say, I think I'm going to ask you a few questions."

The anxious doctor is the problem solver in action: they hear the symptoms, they immediately diagnose, they prescribe the antibiotic. The patient leaves with a prescription — but not with clarity, not with grounding, not with the sense that they have been truly heard.

The present doctor waits. They ask questions. They listen. They give the patient something to relieve the immediate pain — and then they ask them to come back. The patient leaves with a much better outcome.

The analogy is not about being slow or withholding help. It is about the quality of the help. The present doctor gives better help — not less help.

**Soundbite:**
> "You are in control of your dials. Not your conditioning. Not your subconscious programming. You. When do you dial up on problem solving? When do you dial up on presence? That's the work."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Before your next meeting or interaction, set an intention: which dial am I going to turn up today?
2. In the meeting, notice when the problem solving dial starts to turn up automatically. That is the conditioning. You do not have to follow it.
3. Practice the doctor-patient move: when someone brings you a problem, ask one question before you offer a solution. Just one. See what happens.

**Tips, tricks, and nuances:**
- The dials metaphor is most useful in high-stakes situations where the problem solving impulse is strongest. Those are exactly the situations where presence is most valuable.
- The doctor-patient analogy is most useful for change managers and consultants who are used to being the "expert with the answer." The shift to "expert with the question" is uncomfortable at first — but it is the shift that changes the positioning.
- The goal is not to eliminate the problem solving dial. It is to have conscious control over it. There are situations where immediate output is exactly what is needed. The skill is knowing the difference.

**Domain:**
Potential — specifically, the mindset and presence component. Also touches Practice (presence is a practice skill — it requires deliberate development) and Positioning (the shift from output-driven to presence-driven changes how people perceive you).

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Problem Solver Conditioning" earlier in this session — the dials metaphor is the practical tool for overcoming the conditioning.
- Connects to the "Safe Pair of Hands Ceiling" earlier in this session — presence is what breaks the ceiling.
- Connects to the "Shining Light" metaphor from Session 5 — presence is the inner state that enables the shining light.

**Diagram potential:**
Yes. A visual showing two dials: "Problem Solving" and "Presence." Both dials on a spectrum from 0 to 10. A second visual showing the default state (Problem Solving at 9, Presence at 2) vs. the goal state (both at 5, with conscious control over which to turn up based on context).

## 5. The Positioning Shift — From Corporate to Practice (The Menu Problem)

**Source:** Session 7, approx. 05:55–08:50 | Onboarding session

**Verbatim transcript quote:**
> "The biggest shift is when you shift from corporate to having your own solo consulting business. This is a different positioning. It builds on the positioning that you already have. But the way you speak about yourself changes."
> "They talk about themselves exactly the same as they would be doing in an interview. Then they say, why don't I end up getting the gig? Or why, after all this talk, they tell me, you know what, why don't you just come and work with us as a contractor?"
> "In some cases, the positioning is not good at all, in the sense that it's a menu. That's everything I have ever done for the past 20 years. Surely they can see the value. Why am I not getting the role?"

**Pain point this addresses:**
Leaders who are transitioning from corporate to consulting/practice and who are presenting themselves exactly as they would in a job interview — with a comprehensive CV, a long list of skills and experiences, and a "menu" of everything they can do. The result is that clients either do not understand what they do, or they offer them a contractor role instead of the consulting engagement they were pitching for.

**Value add:**
The "menu problem" is one of the most common and most costly positioning mistakes in the GTL Programme. It names the specific failure mode that prevents experienced leaders from landing consulting engagements — and it makes clear that the solution is not more experience or more credentials, but a fundamentally different way of speaking about yourself.

**The menu problem:**
A menu positioning is: "I have 20 years of experience in change management, project management, stakeholder engagement, organisational design, culture change, digital transformation, and leadership development." It is comprehensive. It is accurate. And it is completely ineffective as a consulting positioning.

The problem is not that the person has done all of those things. The problem is that a menu positioning does not tell the client what problem you solve, who you solve it for, or what makes you different from every other experienced leader with a similar menu.

**The corporate-to-practice shift:**
The positioning that works in corporate (comprehensive, broad, "I can do many things") is the positioning that fails in practice (specific, focused, "I solve this specific problem for this specific person"). The shift is not about hiding your experience — it is about curating it. You are not presenting everything you have done; you are presenting the thread that connects everything you have done to the specific problem you solve.

**Soundbite:**
> "The menu is everything you've ever done for 20 years. Surely they can see the value. But they don't. Because a menu is not a positioning. It's a list."

**How-to / Practical application:**
1. Read your current LinkedIn profile or bio. Is it a menu? Or is it a positioning?
2. The test: can someone read your profile and immediately understand (a) what problem you solve, (b) who you solve it for, and (c) what makes you different? If not, it is a menu.
3. The fix: identify the one thread that connects everything you have done. That thread is your positioning. Everything else is context.

**Domain:**
Positioning — specifically, the corporate-to-practice positioning shift. This is the most common positioning failure mode in the GTL Programme.

**Connections to other IP:**
- Connects to the "Three Lenses of Positioning" from Session 4 — the menu problem is the failure to apply the three lenses.
- Connects to the "Positioning Matrix" from Session 5 — the positioning matrix is the tool for moving from a menu to a positioning.

Read it again and tell me what problem you solve."

## Where You Were When We Started

---

## What We Worked Through

### The Distinction Between a Practice and a Business

We began by establishing a foundational distinction that will anchor everything going forward. A **practice** is built on the practice owner's intellectual property — you are the face, the method, and the value. A **business** is when others do the work under your brand. You are building a practice. This means your positioning, your content, your offerings, and your client relationships are all expressions of who you are and what you know.

### The Four Realities of Practice Building

1. **Your why is your anchor.** When it gets hard — and it will — your why is what keeps you going and what shapes the design of your practice.
2. **Practice building is not just delivery.** Content, social media, sales, business development, admin, taxes, video recordings, white papers — this is not optional.
3. **Consistency is non-negotiable.** The algorithm punishes disappearance. Four months of recovery after a six-week break is a real cost.
4. **Network alone will not build a practice.** Referrals sustain you for perhaps a year. After that, you need a brand and positioning that speaks to people who have never met you.

### The Three Lenses of Positioning

**Lens 1 — You.** What do you genuinely care about? What impression do people get from working with you? How would you have people who do not know you say the same thing before they have worked with you?

**Lens 2 — The Work.** What outcomes do you help deliver? What is your method in plain language? What is your edge — the thing that only you do, in the way that only you do it?

**Lens 3 — Them.** Why should clients care? What is at stake for them if they do not solve this problem? The best sales conversation you will ever have is when you articulate the client's problem better than they can articulate it themselves.

### The Bleeding Neck Problem

Your positioning must speak to a **bleeding neck problem** — something so urgent, so high-stakes, that the client cannot afford to wait. Not a nice-to-have.
